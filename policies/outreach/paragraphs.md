# The two middle paragraphs, country by country

These follow the opening presentation of the project, from "In that context, we
have identified the case of...".

The rule taken from the Bahamas letter: we never describe their policy back to
them. The instrument is named inside a clause that says **where we read it** —
"From the regulation X and the notice Y, we identified Z, under which..." — so
that what they read is our source and our reading, not a summary of their own
work. One clause is enough; the detail belongs in the questions.

The second paragraph says why the case matters to us, where our knowledge stops,
what we would like to learn, and asks who could speak to the experience and the
results. No video call is proposed. The lengths differ by country.

---

## Australia — New South Wales

In that context, we have identified the case of New South Wales as particularly
interesting. From the Department's media release of May 2024 and its Returning
teachers page, we identified Teachers Re-Engage, launched in November 2023,
through which the Department approaches teachers who resigned in recent years and
offers them support with re-accreditation and a placement in casual or temporary
work.

We find this initiative valuable because it is one of the very few we have come
across anywhere in which an administration goes back to its own former teachers
one by one, and one of fewer still whose results have been made public. What we
know, however, stops at the figures released in May 2024, and we would like to
learn directly from the Department how it has worked: how the teachers to
approach are identified and how they are contacted, what those conversations
reveal about why they left and what would bring them back, how re-accreditation
is handled with the teaching standards authority, and what has become of those
who returned. If there is a colleague who has followed the initiative closely and
could speak to its experience and its results, we would be very grateful for an
introduction.

## Australia — Victoria

In that context, we have identified the case of Victoria as particularly
interesting. From the Department's pages on the Teacher Re-Engagement Initiative
and the 2023/24 State Budget, we identified the scheme introduced in late 2022,
under which teachers who have been away from the classroom for a year or more are
offered a paid placement in a government school, with coaching and a mentor,
before they apply for a post.

What draws our attention is the sequence: a teacher is paid to try the classroom
again before deciding, rather than asked to decide first, and we have found very
few schemes anywhere built that way. Our understanding stops at the expressions of
interest reported in the first months and at the funding announced through to June
2025, so we would like to learn from the Department how it has gone since: who
came forward and who went on to take a post, how schools and returning teachers
are matched, which part of the offer turns out to matter most, and whether the
initiative has been evaluated. If a colleague who has run it could speak to its
experience and its results, we would be very grateful for the contact.

## Netherlands

In that context, we have identified the case of the Netherlands as particularly
interesting. From the text of the Regeling tegemoetkoming herintreders primair
onderwijs of 2017 and the Ministry's newsletter announcing a further round of
applications in 2019, we identified the grant paid to a primary-school board for
each qualified teacher it took back after at least a year away, on a contract of
six months or more.

We find it valuable because it is the only measure in our set addressed to the
school rather than to the teacher, and because it ran its course and closed, so
the whole story can be told. Our understanding rests on the regulation itself and
we have not found an evaluation, which is why we would like to learn from the
Ministry how it worked: how school boards used the grant, whether it changed their
willingness to consider a candidate who had been out of the classroom, what kind
of teachers came back through it and whether they stayed, and what lay behind the
decision to let the regulation lapse rather than renew it once applications
closed in November 2020. If there is a colleague at the
Ministry, or at the body that handled the applications, who followed the scheme
closely and could speak to its experience and its results, we would be very
grateful if you could point us to them.

## Canada — Quebec

In that context, we have identified the case of Quebec as particularly
interesting. From the Gouvernement du Québec's page on the return to work of
retirees from the education network, and from coverage of the announcement of
September 2020, we identified the financial incentive for the return of retired
teachers carried in the Règles budgétaires de fonctionnement of the centres de
services scolaires — as we understand it, measure 15178 — under which a retired
teacher doing supply work is paid from the first day at the salary step and the
experience held at retirement, where the ordinary rules grant that step only from
the twentieth day, with the pension continuing in full.

We find this case valuable for a reason that sets it apart from the others we have
documented: the instrument is a pay rule inside the budget rules for school
boards, not a programme with its own staff and budget line, and it appears to have
been kept and broadened once the emergency that prompted it had passed, first by
lifting the condition that limited it to teachers who had retired after 2015, then
by making it permanent. Our reading rests on the public page and on press
coverage, as we have not been able to consult the budget rules themselves, so we
would be grateful for a correction if we have the reference or the mechanism
wrong. What we would most like to learn is how it came about and how it has worked
since: why a pay rule was preferred to other ways of bringing retired teachers
back, how it was settled with the parties to teachers' working conditions and on
the pension side, which retired teachers actually came back and for what kind of
work, whether it has changed the way schools cover absences, and whether the
Ministry has assessed it. If a colleague who has followed the measure closely
could speak to us about its experience and its results, an introduction would be
very welcome.

## Portugal

In that context, we have identified the case of Portugal as particularly
interesting. From Decreto-Lei n.º 51/2024 of 28 August and the notice of the
schools' administration directorate on the procedure, we identified the
possibility for a school with an unfilled post in a shortage subject, or one
classed as under-staffed, to contract a teacher who retired five years ago or
less, within a quota fixed each year with the finance ministry.

What interests us is the gap between an offer that is generous on paper, the
pension kept in full and a supplement on top, and a first round in which, as the
press reported, a minority of the posts were filled. That gap is where we think
the lesson lies, and the answers sit in the Ministry's own records rather than in
what has been published. We would like to learn how the quota is set and how
requests from schools are weighed against it, whether the posts filled are those
where the shortage was most acute, what is known about why retired teachers turned
the offer down and what those who accepted were looking for, and how the measure
has been adjusted for the current school year. If a colleague at the Ministry or
at the directorate who has followed it since 2024 could speak to its experience
and its results, an introduction would be very welcome.

## United States — Michigan (only if the US case is kept)

In that context, we have identified the case of Michigan as particularly
interesting. From the guidance published by the Office of Retirement Services on
Public Act 147 of 2023, we identified the change that shortened the period a
retired school employee must wait before being taken back and lifted the ceiling
on what they may earn thereafter, in force until October 2028.

We find it valuable because it is a clean before-and-after in a single state, with
every rehired retiree passing through the retirement system's records, and because
the 2028 expiry obliges the state to weigh up whether it worked. We would like to
learn what has come of it: how many retired teachers have returned and into what
kind of posts, whether the waiting period still holds people back, what has been
observed on the pension side, and what will weigh on the decision when the Act
comes up for renewal. If there is a colleague who could speak to the experience so
far and to what is known of the results, we would be very grateful for an
introduction.
