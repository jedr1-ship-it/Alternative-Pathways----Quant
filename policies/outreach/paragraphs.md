# The two middle paragraphs, country by country

These are the paragraphs that follow the opening presentation of the project,
starting at "In that context, we have identified the case of...". The first
paragraph names the instrument and the year, and says what it does, so that the
ministry can see we have read it. The second says what we would like to learn
and asks who could speak to the experience and the results. There is no mention
of a video call.

Where our reading rests on press rather than on the regulation, the paragraph
says "as we understand it" so that a correction is easy for them to make.

---

## Australia — New South Wales

In that context, we have identified the case of New South Wales and the Teachers
Re-Engage initiative that the Department of Education began in November 2023.
Instead of advertising vacancies, the Department went back through its own
staffing records and approached teachers who had resigned over the previous five
years, one by one, offering help to renew accreditation with the teaching
authority and a placement in casual or temporary work, with a permanent post
possible later on. We have found very few systems anywhere that have gone back
to former teachers in this way, and fewer still that have published what came of
it.

What we would most like to learn is the part that does not appear in the
published material: how the teachers to approach were chosen and how they were
contacted, how those conversations went, what they said had made them leave and
what made coming back possible, and how re-accreditation was handled with the
teaching authority. If there is a colleague who was close to the initiative and
could speak to its experience and its results, we would be very grateful for an
introduction.

## Australia — Victoria

In that context, we have identified the case of Victoria and the Teacher
Re-Engagement Initiative, which began in late 2022 and was funded in the 2023/24
State Budget through to June 2025. Teachers who have been away from the classroom
for at least a year are given up to forty paid days in a government school before
they apply for a post: coaching on applications and on the current curriculum,
lessons observed and lessons taught, and a serving teacher alongside them. We
have come across few programmes that pay people to find out whether they want to
return, rather than asking them to decide first.

We would like to hear how that has worked out: who came forward and who
eventually took a post, how schools were matched with returning teachers and what
they were asked to provide, how much of the forty days turned out to be what
returners actually needed, and what happens to those who decide the classroom is
not for them after all. If the initiative has been evaluated, or if a colleague
who has run it could speak to its experience and its results, we would be very
grateful for the contact.

## Netherlands

In that context, we have identified the case of the Netherlands, and in
particular the Regeling tegemoetkoming herintreders primair onderwijs of 2017,
under which a primary school received up to 2,500 euros for each qualified
teacher it took back who had been out of primary teaching for at least a year, on
a contract of six months or more. What caught our attention is that the money
went to the school board rather than to the teacher, and was meant for the first
months back: guidance, refresher training, time alongside a colleague. It reads
to us as an attempt to take away the cost and the risk a school carries when it
hires someone who has been away, rather than to persuade the teacher. The scheme
ran until January 2020, with a further round of applications in 2019.

We would be very interested to learn how it worked in practice: how schools used
the money, whether it changed their willingness to consider a candidate who had
been out of the classroom, what kind of teachers came back through it and whether
they stayed, and what lay behind the decision not to continue after 2020. If
there is a colleague at the Ministry, or at the body that handled the
applications, who followed the scheme closely and could speak to its experience
and its results, we would be very grateful if you could point us to them.

## Canada — Quebec

In that context, we have identified the case of Quebec, and specifically the
financial incentive for the return of retired teachers that is carried in the
Regles budgetaires de fonctionnement of the centres de services scolaires,
announced in September 2020 and since made permanent. As we understand it, a
retired teacher who takes supply work is paid from the first day at the salary
step and the years of experience held at retirement, where the ordinary rules
grant that step only from the twentieth day of supply teaching, and the pension
continues to be paid in full. The condition limiting the measure to teachers who
had retired after July 2015 was later lifted by ministerial order, and in 2023
the Ministry wrote personally to retired teachers to draw their attention to it.
What interests us is that the instrument is a pay rule inside the budget rules
for school boards rather than a programme with its own staff and budget line, and
that it appears to have been kept once the emergency that prompted it had passed.

We would like to understand how it was arrived at and how it has worked since:
why a pay rule was chosen over other ways of bringing retired teachers back, how
it was settled with the parties to teachers' working conditions and with the
pension side, which retired teachers actually came back and for what kind of
work, and whether the measure has changed the way schools cover absences. We
would also be glad to know whether the Ministry has assessed it. If a colleague
who has followed the measure closely could speak to us about its experience and
its results, an introduction would be very welcome.

## Portugal

In that context, we have identified the case of Portugal and Decree-Law 51/2024,
of August 2024, which lets a school with an unfilled post in a shortage subject,
or a school officially classed as under-staffed, sign a fixed-term contract with
a teacher who retired five years ago or less. The school applies through the
schools' administration directorate and the Ministry authorises the contract
within a quota agreed each year with the finance ministry; the returning teacher
keeps the pension in full and receives an additional payment set against the
first step of the teacher salary scale, in proportion to the hours taught.

We are interested in how a measure of this kind is steered from the centre: how
the quota is set and how requests from schools are weighed against it, whether
the posts that end up being filled are the ones where the shortage was most
acute, how retired teachers responded to the terms on offer, and what the first
year has suggested about the design. If a colleague at the Ministry or at the
directorate who has followed it since 2024 could speak to its experience and its
results, an introduction would be very welcome.

## United States — Michigan (only if the US case is kept)

In that context, we have identified the case of Michigan and Public Act 147 of
2023, signed in October of that year, which shortened from nine months to six the
time a retired school employee must wait before a school can take them back, and
removed the ceiling on what they may earn once that period has passed, so that
the pension continues whatever the salary. The Act runs until October 2028, which
means the state will have to decide whether to keep it.

We would like to learn what the change has produced: how many retired teachers
have come back under it and into what kind of posts, whether the six-month wait
still holds people back, how the retirement system and the districts have
followed the effect on the pension fund, and what will weigh on the decision when
the Act comes up for renewal. If there is a colleague who could speak to the
experience so far and to what is known of the results, we would be very grateful
for an introduction.
