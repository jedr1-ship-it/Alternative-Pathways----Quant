# China
## Silver-Age Lecturing Plan (2018) and the National Silver-Age Teachers Action Plan (2023)

**Who to ask for:** the Teacher Work Department of the Ministry of Education,
which issues the plan with the Ministry of Finance, and, if possible, a
provincial education department that recruits for it.

**What we already know, so we do not ask it.** Since 2018 the Ministry of
Education and the Ministry of Finance have recruited retired principals,
teaching-research officers, special-grade and senior teachers, generally aged 65
or under, to teach in county-town and rural schools in formerly poor, border and
minority counties. Recruitment is organised province by province against named
posts, the county education bureau signs an agreement with the teacher that is
renewed each year, the posting runs for at least one school year, and the teacher
supplies a recent health certificate. The pension and employment status are
untouched and the work is funded at 20,000 yuan per person per year, shared
between central and local budgets, covering an allowance, travel and accident
insurance; the county must provide housing. In 2023 ten government departments
extended the model to higher, vocational and private education, with a target of
around 120,000 silver-age teachers within roughly three years. The annual plan
has been 7,000 teachers for compulsory education in both 2024 and 2025.

---

### Opening

1. Could you tell us how the plan started in 2018, and what problem it was meant
   to solve that other measures for rural schools were not solving?

2. In 2023 it grew into something much larger, across ten departments and all
   levels of education. What changed in the thinking between 2018 and 2023?

### How it works

3. Could you describe how a retired teacher goes from hearing about the plan to
   standing in a classroom in a county far from home?

4. Who decides which counties and which schools receive a teacher, and how is
   that demand established?

5. What does a silver-age teacher actually do in the school week — teaching,
   working with the young teachers, advising the school leadership? How does that
   balance settle in practice?

6. The agreement is renewed each year. What does the county look at when it
   decides to renew, and how long do teachers tend to stay?

7. The plan pays an allowance on top of the pension rather than a salary, and the
   county provides housing. How was that arrangement arrived at, and does it work
   for the teachers and for the counties?

### The teachers

8. Who applies? We would like to understand what moves a retired principal or
   senior teacher to spend a year in a rural county.

9. What do they find hardest — the distance from family, the health of working at
   that age, the difference between the schools they knew and the ones they
   arrive in?

10. What do the young teachers in those schools get from having them there?

### Results and what comes next

11. How has recruitment gone against the annual plan? We understand the notices
    ask provinces to raise their completion rate, and we would like to hear where
    the difficulty lies.

12. The 2023 plan sets a figure of around 120,000. How is progress measured, and
    where does it stand?

13. Has anyone looked at what the plan has changed in the receiving schools?

14. What would you say a country with an ageing teaching profession should take
    from this, and what should it avoid?

### Close

15. Is there a colleague at the ministry, or at a provincial education department
    that has recruited for the plan, who could speak to its experience and its
    results? We would be very grateful for an introduction.
