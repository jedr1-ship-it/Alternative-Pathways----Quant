# China
## Silver-Age Lecturing Plan (2018) and the National Silver-Age Teachers Action Plan (2023)

**Who to ask for:** the Teacher Work Department of the Ministry of Education,
which issues the plan with the Ministry of Finance, and, if possible, a
provincial education department that recruits for it.

**What we already know, so we do not ask it.** Since 2018 the Ministry of
Education and the Ministry of Finance have recruited retired principals,
teaching-research officers, special-grade and senior teachers, generally aged 65
or under, to county-town and rural schools in formerly poor, border and minority
counties. Recruitment runs province by province against named posts; the county
education bureau signs an agreement with the teacher, renewed each year, for at
least one school year, and the teacher supplies a recent health certificate. The
pension is untouched and the work is funded at 20,000 yuan per person per year,
shared between central and local budgets, covering an allowance, travel and
accident insurance; the county provides housing. In 2023 ten government
departments extended the model to higher, vocational and private education, with
a target of around 120,000 silver-age teachers within roughly three years. The
annual plan has been 7,000 teachers for compulsory education in both 2024 and
2025, and the annual notices ask provinces to raise their completion rate.

1. **Could you tell us how the plan started in 2018, and what it was meant to
   do that other measures for rural schools were not doing?**
   - Why retired teachers, rather than more posts for young ones?
   - In 2023 it grew into something much larger. What changed in the thinking?

2. **Could you describe how a retired teacher goes from hearing about the plan
   to standing in a classroom in a county far from home?**
   - Who decides which counties and schools receive a teacher, and how is that
     demand established?
   - How are applicants chosen when there are more, or fewer, than the posts?

3. **What does a silver-age teacher actually do in the school week?**
   - Teaching, working with the young teachers, advising the school leadership
     — how does that balance settle in practice?
   - What do the young teachers in those schools get from having them there?

4. **The plan pays an allowance on top of the pension, the county provides
   housing, and the agreement is renewed each year. How was that arrangement
   arrived at, and does it work for the teachers and for the counties?**
   - What does a county look at when it decides to renew?
   - How long do teachers tend to stay?

5. **Who applies?** We would like to understand what moves a retired principal or
   senior teacher to spend a year in a rural county.
   - What do they find hardest — distance from family, health, the difference
     between the schools they knew and the ones they arrive in?
   - What do those who leave early say?

6. **When you think about why teachers leave rural schools, or never go to
   them, is the silver-age teacher an answer to that, or a different kind of
   answer?** This is the question our chapter turns on.
   - Does a retired teacher in the school change anything about whether the
     young ones stay?

7. **How has recruitment gone against the annual plan, and where does the
   difficulty lie?**
   - The 2023 plan sets a figure of around 120,000. How is progress measured,
     and where does it stand?
   - Has anyone looked at what the plan has changed in the receiving schools?

8. **What would you say a country with an ageing teaching profession should take
   from this, and what should it avoid?**

9. **Is there a colleague at the ministry, or at a provincial education
   department that has recruited for the plan, who could speak to the
