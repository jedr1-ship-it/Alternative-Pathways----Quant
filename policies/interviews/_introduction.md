# Introduction

Teacher shortages have led a number of education systems to look for supply
beyond initial teacher education. One source has received little systematic
attention: qualified teachers who are no longer teaching. In most systems this is
a large group, larger in several countries than the annual output of teacher
education, and it is made up of three distinct populations — teachers who moved
to another occupation, teachers who left the labour market, and teachers who
retired. Policies that seek to bring any of them back to the classroom are the
subject of the chapter to which this interview contributes, part of the project
Unlocking the Potential of Diverse Teaching Profiles at the OECD Centre for
Educational Research and Innovation.

The chapter maps these policies across countries and classifies them by the
instrument they rely on: financial incentives, information and nudges, support
and training, flexible positions, and flexible re-certification. Documents tell
us the design. They rarely tell us take-up, retention or cost, and evaluations
are scarce. What we are after in these conversations is the experience of the
people who designed and ran the measures: what a design was meant to achieve,
what happened once it was in place, and what they would now do differently.

A second line of the research concerns the reasons teachers leave. The attrition
literature points consistently to working conditions, workload and career
prospects, and less uniformly to pay. Policies that bring teachers back tend to
act on something else — on the cost of returning, on information, on the terms
of a part-time or temporary post. Whether the two meet is an open question, and
one we mean to examine case by case: whom a measure actually brings back, and
whether they are the teachers the system loses.

The conversation follows a small number of open questions. It is not an
evaluation. Nothing said will be attributed to you without your agreement, and
any question can be left aside.
