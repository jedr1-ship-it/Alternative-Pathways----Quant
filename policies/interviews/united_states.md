# United States
## State laws letting retired teachers return to work

**Who to ask for:** in each state, two bodies rather than one — the teachers'
retirement system, which administers the return and holds the records, and the
department of education or the certification board, which controls the licence.
That split is itself part of the story.

**What we already know, so we do not ask it.** Almost every state lets a retired
teacher come back under conditions, and the conditions are built from the same
four levers: a waiting period before returning, a ceiling on earnings, a
shortage designation that the district must establish, and a limit on how long
the return may last. The states in our set have moved these levers in different
directions and at different times: Michigan shortened the wait and removed the
earnings ceiling afterwards, under a law that expires in 2028; Georgia opened
full-time return only to teachers with thirty years of service in subjects a
region had designated as highest-need, and its own audit found the measure had
little effect; New Mexico lets a retiree draw pension and full salary with no
earnings or hours cap, for a limited number of months, with each return approved
by the retirement board; Louisiana removed the cut-off that had restricted its
critical-shortage route to people who retired before 2020; North Carolina ran the
most generous version of all between 1999 and 2009 and then let it lapse;
Virginia went at the licence rather than the pension.

---

### Questions for any of the states

**Origin and design**

1. Could you tell us how this came about in your state, and what problem it was
   meant to solve?

2. The rules in this area are built from a few levers — how long someone waits,
   how much they may earn, whether the district must show a shortage, how long
   the return may last. Which of those were argued over most, and why did they
   land where they did?

3. Who had to be persuaded: the legislature, the retirement system, the
   districts, the unions?

**How it works**

4. What does a retiree actually have to do to come back, and who decides whether
   they may?

5. Where does the paperwork slow people down?

6. Licensing sits with a different body from the pension. For someone whose
   certificate has lapsed, how much of an obstacle is that in practice, and does
   anyone own that part of the problem?

7. What do districts have to demonstrate before they can hire a retiree, and is
   that test a real filter or a formality?

**The teachers and the schools**

8. Who comes back, into what kind of posts, and in which schools? We are
   particularly interested in whether they end up where the shortage is worst.

9. What do retirees say when they decide not to come back?

10. Is there a concern that returning retirees take positions that would
    otherwise go to teachers starting out — and has anyone looked at whether that
    happens?

**Results and what comes next**

11. How many have returned, and how do you count them? We have found it hard to
    get a figure that means the same thing from one state to the next.

12. What has it cost the retirement system, and how is that judged?

13. Has the measure been evaluated or audited, and what did that conclude?

14. Several of these laws have an expiry date. What will decide whether yours is
    kept, and what would you want to know before that decision?

15. If you were advising another state, what would you tell them to do
    differently?

### State-specific follow-ups

**Michigan** — the waiting period was shortened and the earnings ceiling after it
removed, with the law running to October 2028. How did that change behaviour, is
the six-month wait still what holds people back, and what will the decision to
extend turn on? How does the requirement of a genuine separation from the
employer work in practice, given that it also reaches people hired through an
agency?

**Georgia** — the state audit found that only a few hundred teachers a year came
back, and that the one-year wait, more than the pay rules, was what put people
off. The legislature has since widened eligibility and extended the measure, but
we understand the one-year wait for full-time return stayed. Could you tell us
how that was decided? And how did regions actually designate the highest-need
subjects that the original law required?

**New Mexico** — every return goes through the retirement board. What does that
approval consist of, how many have been approved, and what does the board see
that nobody else does? What happens to a teacher when the months allowed run out?

**Louisiana** — the change that opened the critical-shortage route to everyone,
regardless of when they retired, looks small on paper and large in effect. What
did it change? And what does a district have to do to certify a shortage?

**North Carolina** — the policy of 1999 to 2009 is the one case with published
research behind it, and the finding is that returning retirees went
disproportionately to high-need schools. Why was it allowed to lapse, and what
would it take to revisit it?

**Virginia** — yours is the one case in our set that went at the licence rather
than the pension. What did that change for retirees specifically, and where do
things stand now?

---

**Note for the interviewer.** These state summaries were assembled from
retirement-system and legislature sources that we could read only in part. Before
sending, check the current rules in the state concerned — several were amended in
2025 and 2026 — and treat anything in the state-specific section as a question
rather than a statement.
