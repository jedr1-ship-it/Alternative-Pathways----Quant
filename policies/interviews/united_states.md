# United States
## State laws letting retired teachers return to work

**Who to ask for:** in each state, two bodies rather than one — the teachers'
retirement system, which administers the return and holds the records, and the
department of education or the certification board, which controls the licence.
That split is part of the story.

**What we already know, so we do not ask it.** Almost every state lets a retired
teacher come back under conditions, and the conditions are built from the same
four levers: a waiting period before returning, a ceiling on earnings, a shortage
designation the district must establish, and a limit on how long the return may
last. The states in our set have moved these levers in different directions.
Michigan shortened the wait to six months and removed the earnings ceiling after
it, under a law that expires in October 2028. Georgia opened full-time return
only to teachers with thirty years of service, in subjects a region had
designated as highest-need, and its own audit in 2025 found the measure had
little effect — a few hundred teachers a year — with the one-year wait, not the
pay rules, as the main deterrent; the legislature has since widened eligibility
and extended the measure to 2034. New Mexico lets a retiree draw pension and
full salary with no cap on earnings or hours, for a limited number of months,
each return approved by the retirement board. Louisiana removed the cut-off that
had restricted its critical-shortage route to people who retired before mid-2020.
North Carolina ran the most generous version of all between 1999 and 2009, and
published research finds those returners went disproportionately to high-need
schools; the policy was allowed to lapse. Virginia went at the licence rather
than the pension. Several of these laws were amended in 2025 and 2026: check the
current text before the conversation.

---

### Questions for any of the states

1. **Could you tell us how this came about in your state, and what problem it
   was meant to solve?**
   - Who had to be persuaded: the legislature, the retirement system, the
     districts, the unions?
   - Was there another state you were looking at?

2. **The rules here are built from a few levers — how long someone waits, how
   much they may earn, whether the district must show a shortage, how long the
   return may last. Which of those were argued over most, and why did they land
   where they did?**
   - Is the waiting period a pension question, a federal-tax question, or a
     political one?

3. **What does a retiree actually have to do to come back, and who decides
   whether they may?**
   - Where does the paperwork slow people down?
   - Licensing sits with a different body from the pension. For someone whose
     certificate has lapsed, how much of an obstacle is that, and does anyone
     own that part of the problem?
   - What must a district show before it can hire a retiree — a real filter or
     a formality?

4. **Who comes back, into what kind of posts, and in which schools?** We are
   particularly interested in whether they end up where the shortage is worst.
   - What do retirees say when they decide not to come back?

5. **When you think about why teachers leave your state's schools, is a returning
   retiree an answer to that, or an answer to something else?** This is the
   question our chapter turns on.
   - Is there a concern that returning retirees take positions that would
     otherwise go to teachers starting out — and has anyone looked?

6. **How many have returned, and how do you count them?** We have found it hard
   to get a figure that means the same thing from one state to the next.
   - What has it cost the retirement system, and how is that judged?
   - Has the measure been evaluated or audited, and what did that conclude?

7. **Several of these laws have an expiry date. What will decide whether yours is
   kept, and what would you want to know before that decision?**
   - If you were advising another state, what would you tell them to do
     differently?

8. **Is there a colleague, at the retirement system or on the certification side,
   who could speak to the experience and the results?** We would be very
   grateful for an introduction.

### One follow-up per state

- **Michigan.** Is the six-month wait still what holds people back, and how does
  the requirement of a genuine separation from the employer work in practice,
  given that it also reaches people hired through an agency?
- **Georgia.** The audit named the one-year wait as the main deterrent, and we
  understand the new law kept it while widening eligibility. How was that
  decided?
- **New Mexico.** Every return goes through the retirement board. What does that
  approval consist of, and what happens to a teacher when the months allowed run
  out?
- **Louisiana.** Removing the retirement-date cut-off looks small on paper and
  large in effect. What did it change, and what does a district have to do to
  certify a shortage?
- **North Carolina.** Yours is the one case with published research behind it.
  Why was the policy allowed to lapse, and what would it take to revisit it?
- **Virginia.** Yours went at the licence rather than the pension. What did that
  change for retirees specifically, and where do things stand now?
