# Netherlands
## Regeling tegemoetkoming herintreders primair onderwijs (2017–2022)

**Who to ask for:** the primary-education directorate of the Ministry of
Education, Culture and Science, and the grant agency that handled the
applications (DUS-I).

**What we already know, so we do not ask it.** The regulation took effect on
1 November 2017 and paid a primary-school board up to 2,500 euros for each
qualified teacher it took on who had not worked in primary education for at least
a year, on a contract of six months or more. The money went to the board, not the
teacher, for the guidance and support a returner needs in the first months. The
first budget was 1.25 million euros, enough for about 500 returners; a 2019
amendment extended the scheme, applications were accepted until 1 November 2020,
and the regulation expired on 1 January 2022. In mid-2019 the ministry reported
that more than a hundred returners had used it, against a pool of qualified
teachers outside education that the ministry itself put in the tens of thousands.
A Dutch teaching qualification does not expire, so there is no re-licensing step
on the way back.

---

1. **Could you tell us how the scheme came about in 2017, and what the thinking
   was in paying the school rather than the teacher?**
   - Was that design debated at the time? What would the alternative have been?
   - Who inside the ministry, or outside it, pushed for it?

2. **What did a school board actually have to do to claim it, and what did they
   spend the money on?**
   - Did 2,500 euros change a board's decision, or did they hire the person
     anyway and claim afterwards?

3. **Our reading of the figures is that a good deal less of the money was used
   than was available. Is that right, and what was behind it?**
   - Was the scheme known to schools?
   - Did the six-month contract or the twelve-month absence rule get in the
     way?

4. **In 2019 the scheme was extended rather than dropped, and then it ran out at
   the beginning of 2022 without being replaced by anything of the same kind.
   How were those two decisions made?**
   - Was it evaluated, and what did it teach you?
   - Did the work move into the regional partnerships, and does anyone there
     still target returners specifically?

5. **Who are the herintreders, in your experience?** We are aware the definition
   also covers people who qualified and never started.
   - What do they say about why they left primary teaching, and about what
     makes coming back possible?
   - Does the fact that a qualification never expires make returning easier, or
     does it simply move the difficulty somewhere else?

6. **When you think about why primary teachers leave — workload, class size,
   leadership — does a grant to the school reach that, or something else?** This
   is the question our chapter turns on.
   - The number of returners in primary education has fallen over the last
     decade while the shortage continued. How do you read that?

7. **The debate about the silent reserve now seems to be about whether that pool
   is really available, given how many are already working elsewhere. Where has
   the ministry landed, and what follows from it for policy?**
   - Do you know whether the teachers who came back through the scheme stayed?

8. **Is there a colleague at the ministry, or at the agency that handled the
   applications, who followed the scheme closely and could speak to the
   experience and the results?** We would be grateful if you could point us to
   them.
