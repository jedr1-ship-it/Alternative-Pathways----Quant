# Audit: "Where dedicated instruments exist, they tend to be restricted and infrequent"

Verification of the proposal claim across the seven CPF 2.0 countries. Compiled July 2026 from primary sources (statistical agencies, data-access policies, published release calendars). Full source URLs at bottom of each country block in the underlying research notes.

## Verdict by country

## The claim that survives scrutiny

1. Frequency is NOT the general problem. Five of seven countries publish an aggregate attrition figure every year (UK, Germany, Korea, Netherlands, Australia since 2021). Only the US national survey (8 waves in 35 years, one 9-year gap) and Switzerland's attrition analyses (one-off LABB studies) are genuinely infrequent.
2. Microdata access IS the general problem. Person-level records are gated everywhere: restricted-use licenses limited to US institutions (NCES), secure enclaves (Texas ERC, ONS SRS, AIHW, ABS DataLab), negotiated contracts (BFS, FDZ), or simply not released (Korea). The two genuine exceptions are Washington State's S-275 public files and the Netherlands' routinized CBS Remote Access.
3. Analytical depth is the deepest gap. No public tier anywhere carries the covariates, the destinations, or the returns. The only dedicated instrument that follows leavers to their destinations (the US TFS) is precisely the infrequent, restricted one.
4. The household panels are the mirror image: openly accessible (UKHLS, HILDA, KLIPS, SOEP, SHP, LISS, PSID all via standard registration), annual, rich in covariates — but not teacher-dedicated and small. This is the gap the CPF proposal occupies.

## Recommended wording (implemented in the proposal)

"Most countries now publish an aggregate attrition rate each year from administrative records. What is scarce is everything behind the rate: person-level records sit in secure enclaves, closed registers or restricted licenses, and the public tables carry no personal characteristics, no destinations and no returns. The one national survey that follows leavers to where they go, the American Teacher Follow-up Survey, has run eight times since 1988, with a nine-year gap before its latest wave, and its recent microdata are available only to licensed institutions in the United States."
