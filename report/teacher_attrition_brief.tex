{\bfseries\color{ecnavy}EXECUTIVE SUMMARY}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=14pt,itemsep=2pt]
  \item Linking individuals across the CPS rotation design, \textbf{24\% of school
  teachers observed in 2024 were no longer teaching twelve months later}: 16.3
  percentage points (pp) moved to another occupation, 6.5\,pp left the labor
  force and 1.2\,pp were unemployed.
  \item \textbf{Working part-time is the strongest single predictor} of leaving
  ($+18$\,pp), while holding a graduate degree is the strongest protective
  factor ($-20$\,pp). Exit risk is U-shaped in age, lowest around age 40.
  \item The \textbf{typical leaver} is female, in her early fifties or her
  twenties, teaching part-time, without a graduate credential, and
  disproportionately in preschool/kindergarten; teachers in the top predicted-risk
  decile face a 59\% exit probability, 2.5 times the average.
  \item Retention policy should focus on \textbf{converting involuntary part-time
  positions into full-time contracts and subsidizing graduate credentials} for
  the least-attached segments of the teaching workforce.
\end{itemize}
\end{tcolorbox}

\section*{\color{ecnavy}1. Data: a 12-month panel of teachers}

We use official \textbf{Current Population Survey (CPS) basic monthly
microdata} (U.S. Census Bureau), January 2024 to December 2025 (23 monthly
files; October 2025 was not released). Exploiting the CPS 4--8--4 rotation
design, each adult interviewed in 2024 is re-interviewed in the same calendar
month of 2025 and can be linked by household and person identifiers, with
matches validated on sex, race and age progression (Madrian--Lefgren
criterion). This yields \textbf{298{,}586 validated individual links}, of which
\textbf{6{,}426 were school teachers} at baseline (preschool to secondary,
including special education; Census occupation codes 2300--2330). A
\emph{leaver} is a baseline teacher who, twelve months later, is not employed
as a school teacher. Reported rates use CPS final person weights.

\textit{Caveat:} occupation in the returning rotation is re-coded
independently, which inflates measured occupation switching; the 24\% level
should be read as an upper bound, while \emph{comparisons across groups} --- the
object of this brief --- are far less affected.

\section*{\color{ecnavy}2. Who leaves: raw differences}

Attrition is heavily concentrated in three margins: \textbf{hours},
\textbf{credentials} and \textbf{teaching level}. Part-time teachers leave at
more than twice the rate of full-time teachers; teachers without a bachelor's
degree leave at nearly three times the rate of those with a master's; and
preschool/kindergarten teachers are the most exposed level.

\section*{\color{ecnavy}3. A probit model of leaving teaching}

We estimate a probit for the probability of leaving, with all covariates
measured at baseline and standard errors clustered by household
(Table~\ref{tab:probit}). Figure~\ref{fig:ame} plots the average marginal
effects. Three results stand out. First, \textbf{part-time status raises exit
probability by 18\,pp}, the largest effect in the model --- attachment to
full-time hours, not demographics, is the dominant margin. Second,
\textbf{credentials protect}: a bachelor's degree lowers exit risk by 18\,pp
and a graduate degree by 20\,pp relative to teachers without a degree,
consistent with higher occupation-specific investment raising the cost of
leaving. Third, \textbf{minority and non-citizen teachers are significantly
more likely to leave} ($+6$ to $+11$\,pp), pointing to a retention gap with
direct implications for workforce diversity.

Age enters with a robust U-shape (Figure~\ref{fig:age}): predicted risk falls
through the twenties and thirties, bottoms out at 18\% around age 40, and
accelerates after 55 as retirement margins open --- the familiar U-shaped
attrition profile of the teacher-turnover literature.

\section*{\color{ecnavy}4. The profile of the typical leaver}

Ranking teachers by predicted risk, the top decile --- with a mean exit
probability of 59\%, against 23\% for the average teacher --- is
overwhelmingly female (86\%), five times more likely to work part-time (55\%
vs.\ 11\%), three times less likely to hold a graduate degree (15\% vs.\
50\%), and four times more concentrated in preschool/kindergarten (40\% vs.\
11\%), with Hispanic and Black teachers over-represented.

\section*{\color{ecnavy}5. Policy implications}

The results locate teacher attrition in \textbf{weak attachment} --- part-time
hours, missing credentials, the early-childhood segment --- rather than in
broad demographics. Three levers follow directly: (i) \textbf{full-time
conversion} of involuntary part-time teaching positions; (ii)
\textbf{credentialing subsidies} (tuition support for master's programs)
targeted at non-credentialed teachers, whose exit risk is highest; and (iii)
\textbf{retention packages for preschool and kindergarten teachers}, the level
where pay and conditions gaps versus other levels are largest. The
significant minority and non-citizen retention gaps warrant specific
monitoring, since they compound into the diversity of the future teaching
workforce.
