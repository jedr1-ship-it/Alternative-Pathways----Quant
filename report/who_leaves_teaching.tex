\begin{center}\bfseries Abstract\end{center}\vspace{2pt}
One in six American teachers supposedly walks out the door every year.
That number counts teachers who change schools and keep teaching; the
share who actually leave the profession is half as large, and it is not
rising. I measure it with one instrument pushed as far as it will go:
the March Current Population Survey question comparing last year's
longest job with this week's occupation, read from the raw Census files
for twenty-eight consecutive years, 1997--2024. Leaving teaching
averages 8.6 percent per year. It peaked at 10.3 percent in 2001 and at
10.2 percent in 2019, and stood at 7.7 percent in 2024, below its
long-run mean. The anatomy of the flow explains its stability. Of every
100 leavers, 62 are not working the following March---38 of them past
age 55, on their way out of the labor force for good---and only 26 hold
another job. Booms do not lure teachers away: where exits respond to
the business cycle at all, recessions push them out. Career changers
face a gamble rather than a raise---the median switcher gains 15 log
points, a quarter lose more than 30, and nearly half of all leavers
earn nothing the following year. What predicts leaving is the contract,
not the person: part-year work, part-time hours, and missing pension
coverage dwarf every demographic. The one personal event that matters
is a birth, which raises a woman's exit probability by six percentage
points, twice the effect in nursing---and that margin has been fading
for two decades as the motherhood exit disappears. Teacher pay slid
from 80 to 72 percent of the median college graduate over the window;
teaching is nonetheless the only comparison profession whose attrition
fell. Teachers do leave---for retirement and for family, at the rate
they always have. The exodus the debate keeps rediscovering is not in
the data.}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Every few years the United States rediscovers that its teachers are
quitting. The discovery always arrives with a statistic---most
recently, that one in six teachers walks away every year---and the
statistic always carries the same message: this time it is an exodus.
The concern behind the alarm is legitimate. Turnover is costly for
schools and students \citep{ronfeldt2013,hanushek2004}, staffing is
chronically hard in special education, mathematics, and high-poverty
schools \citep{carverthomas2017}, and teacher pay has fallen visibly
behind other college-graduate careers \citep{allegretto2023}. But the
statistic is not what it seems. Sixteen percent counts two very
different behaviors as leaving: teachers who change schools and keep
teaching, and teachers who leave the classroom altogether. Only the
second group belongs in a sentence containing the word exodus---and
that group has been seven to eight percent of the workforce for as
long as any dataset can see.

This paper measures that group longer and deeper than it has been
measured before. The March Current Population Survey asks every
household two simple questions: what was the longest job you held last
calendar year, and what do you do this week? Whoever taught last year
and no longer teaches this March has left teaching within a window of
at most fifteen months---and the survey then records where they landed
(another occupation, unemployment, or out of the labor force) and what
they earn. \citet{harris2007} introduced this design; \citet{aldeman2025}
recently used its modern files to establish the level; the school-roster
surveys of the National Center for Education Statistics confirm the same
level independently \citep{nces2024}. What no one has done is run the
instrument at full depth and full length. I rebuild every supplement
from 1998 to 2025 from the raw Census files, harmonize them into a
single database of five million person-year records, and validate the
instrument person by person against panel classifications. I then
follow every exit: by route, by age, by sex, by sector, by state, by
family event, and---linking consecutive Marches---by what it pays. The
result is a twenty-eight-year anatomy of who leaves teaching, where
they go, and why.

The headline is that there is no trend. Leaving teaching averaged 8.6
percent over 1997--2024, with two nearly identical peaks a generation
apart---10.3 percent in 2001, at the end of the dot-com boom, and 10.2
in 2019, on the eve of the pandemic---and a 2024 reading of 7.7, below
the long-run mean (Figure~\ref{fig:evolution}). Covid produced a
two-year excursion that fully reversed. The rate is stable because of
what it is made of, and its composition is the story of this paper.

Most leaving is not career change. Of every 100 leavers, 62 are not
working the following March---38 of them at ages 55 or older, the
retirement route---and only 26 hold another job, a third of those
still in education and care (Figure~\ref{fig:flow100}). The exits that
remain respond to life, not to the labor market. A new baby raises a
woman's probability of leaving by 5.9 percentage points, roughly
double the effect in nursing or accounting
(Figure~\ref{fig:newbaby})---though that margin has been closing for
twenty-five years as the motherhood exit fades from American labor
supply (Figure~\ref{fig:mothers}). A booming economy, by contrast,
does not lure teachers away. Across twenty-eight years and fifty-one
jurisdictions, every statistically detectable cyclical response says
recessions push teachers out; none says booms pull them away
(Figures~\ref{fig:cycle} and~\ref{fig:states}).

Nor does leaving pay---or it pays like a lottery. The median career
changer gains 15 log points, but a quarter lose more than 30, and 47
percent of all leavers report no earnings at all the following year
(Figure~\ref{fig:density}). A risk-averse, pension-covered workforce
takes few such gambles, which is what the flat series shows. Meanwhile
the pay of those who stay slid from 80 to 72 percent of the median
college graduate (Figure~\ref{fig:paywindows})---and teaching is
nonetheless the only comparison profession whose attrition fell
between the first and second halves of the sample
(Figure~\ref{fig:professions}).

The paper is organized around its figures. Section~\ref{sec:data}
describes the instrument, the pooled database, the definitions that
reconcile the numbers in the public debate, and the validation
exercises. Sections~\ref{sec:workforce}--\ref{sec:professions} present
the anatomy: the changing workforce, the rate, the routes, the family
margin, the cycle, pay, the multivariate portrait, and the comparison
professions. Section~\ref{sec:policy} concludes with what the anatomy
implies for policy. Appendices carry the instrument audit, the
comparison with \citet{harris2007}, construction details, robustness
tables, and additional figures.

\section{Data and Measurement}\label{sec:data}

\subsection{The March Supplement}

The Annual Social and Economic Supplement (ASEC) of the CPS, fielded
every March, asks two questions that together identify profession
leaving. The first is retrospective: the occupation of the longest job
held during the previous calendar year. The second is contemporaneous:
the occupation of the job held this week, together with a recode of
labor force status that distinguishes the employed, the unemployed,
and those outside the labor force. A teacher is a respondent whose
longest job last year carried a K--12 teaching occupation code
(preschool through secondary and special education, plus the ``other
teachers'' residual; postsecondary teachers, aides, and tutors are
excluded). A leaver is a teacher who, by the March interview, is not
employed in a teaching occupation: working in some other occupation
(a switcher), unemployed, or out of the labor force. Because status
comes from the labor force recode rather than from the occupation
variable---which carries the last job held even for the
unemployed---the three routes are mutually exclusive and exhaustive.

Three features of the design matter for interpretation and recur
throughout the paper. \textbf{Timing.} The supplement of survey year
$t{+}1$ measures calendar year $t$; the exit itself occurs at some
point between the end of the teaching spell in year $t$ and the March
interview---for teachers, typically at the end of a school year. I
therefore date exits by the calendar year taught and, where the labor
market at the moment of observation matters
(Section~\ref{sec:cycle}), measure it in the survey month itself.
\textbf{Leave is not exit.} A teacher on maternity or medical leave in
March reports ``with a job, not at work,'' counts as employed,
and---if the job is a teaching job---as a stayer. The leavers counted
here are genuine separations. \textbf{Movers are invisible by design.}
A teacher who changes school but keeps teaching keeps the occupation
code and is a stayer. This is precisely what makes the CPS number a
measure of profession attrition rather than school turnover.

\subsection{Sample}

The analysis pools all twenty-eight supplements, survey years
1998--2025: 5,009,129 person-year records, of which 104,545 are
teacher-years and 85,497 belong to the baseline universe of
college-graduate teachers aged 18 and over. Building this sample is
the paper's main measurement investment. The modern files
(2011--2025) are machine-readable and enter directly. The older files
(1998--2010) must be read position by position from the original
fixed-width records; I extract every variable at the positions the
Census technical documentation publishes, and I subject every
extraction to validation gates---age distributions, weight positivity,
teacher shares of employment---that abort rather than ship on failure.
The payoff is a dataset in which education, weights, labor force
status, demographics, household structure, the characteristics of last
year's job (weeks, hours, class of worker, pension coverage),
earnings, and state of residence exist on the same footing in every
single year, and in which occupation codes are harmonized across the
four Census classification regimes.
Appendix~\ref{app:construction} documents the construction;
Table~\ref{tab:sources} reports record counts by source. All estimates
are weighted with the ASEC supplement weight unless noted.

\subsection{Definitions}
\label{sec:definitions}

The numbers in the public debate are all correct and all measure
different things; the alarm comes from mixing them. The NCES Teacher
Follow-up Survey reports total turnover of public school teachers of
13--16 percent across its waves. That total is the sum of two
components the survey itself keeps separate: movers, who change school
but keep teaching (5--8 percent per wave), and leavers, who exit the
profession (5.6 percent in 1988--89, rising to 7.4--8.4 percent from
2000--01 onward) \citep{nces2024,tan2025}. The CPS estimate of roughly
7.6 percent in \citet{aldeman2025} measures leavers only, as does the
7.7 percent of \citet{harris2007} for 1992--2001, and as does the
series built here, which averages 8.2 percent for college-graduate
teachers over 2015--2024 and 8.6 percent over the full window. There
is no contradiction anywhere in this literature: school-to-school
mobility roughly doubles profession leaving, and only the latter
belongs in a sentence containing the word exodus. My estimates sit
slightly above the NCES leaver rates for overlapping years, as they
should: the CPS covers private school teachers, whose attrition is
nearly twice the public rate (Section~\ref{sec:routes}), while the
NCES figure is public-only.

\subsection{Validation}\label{sec:validation}

Two audits back the instrument. First, a person-level
cross-validation: for respondents observed in the CPS monthly rotation
long enough to be classified by a four-month within-panel criterion
and who also answer the March retrospective question, the two
classifications agree in 99.1 percent of cases ($n=9{,}461$;
Appendix~\ref{app:instrument}, Table~\ref{tab:confusion}). Second, a
timing audit: the correlation of the leaving series with the national
unemployment rate is $+0.08$ using the year before the teaching year,
$+0.32$ using the teaching year itself, and $+0.47$ using the year the
exit is observed---exactly the gradient the retrospective design
implies, and evidence that the instrument dates transitions where it
claims to. External anchors point the same way: the NCES leaver rates
for its five overlapping waves fall within a point of the series.

\section{The Teaching Workforce}\label{sec:workforce}

Figure~\ref{fig:workforce} tracks the composition of the teaching
workforce over the last two decades, panel by panel; each panel shows
the annual series in light gray and a three-year average in blue. Four
slow-moving facts stand out, and each returns later in the paper as a
mechanical driver of attrition.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/g1_workforce.pdf}
\caption{Composition of the Teaching Workforce, 2005--2024}
\label{fig:workforce}
\fignote{\textit{Notes:} College-graduate teachers aged 18+ whose
longest job last year was K--12 teaching; ASEC supplement weights.
Thin gray lines are annual values; blue lines are centered 3-year
averages, with endpoint values printed. ``Public school,''
``part-time,'' and ``pension plan'' refer to the longest job held last
year; the open circle marks 2013, a year with a reduced sample due to
the ASEC redesign. \textit{Source:} Author's calculations from March
CPS supplements, survey years 2006--2025.}
\end{figure}

The workforce is aging: mean age rose from 42.8 to 44.8, and the share
of teachers aged 55 or more rose with it---which matters because
Section~\ref{sec:routes} shows the 55+ retirement route is the largest
single component of attrition. It is increasingly credentialed (45 to
51 percent with a master's degree or more) and slowly diversifying (8
to 11 percent Black). The share with a young child at home has fallen
from 19 to 14 percent, the demographic echo of later and lower
fertility---and, as Section~\ref{sec:family} shows, the family exit
route has fallen even faster than the share of potential mothers.
Finally, the employment contract has deteriorated on the one margin
the attrition model will single out: pension coverage at the last-year
job fell from 80 to 66 percent, the steepest change in any panel.

\section{The Rate of Leaving, 1997--2024}
\label{sec:evolution}

Figure~\ref{fig:evolution} presents the paper's central estimate: the
share of college-graduate teachers who left the profession each
calendar year from 1997 to 2024, on one continuous, consistently
defined, weighted series---to my knowledge the longest ever assembled
from this instrument.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{figures/g2_evolution.pdf}
\caption{Leaving Teaching, 1997--2024}
\label{fig:evolution}
\fignote{\textit{Notes:} Share of college-graduate teachers (longest
job last calendar year) not employed in a teaching occupation the
following March, by route-based definition; ASEC weights. Shaded band:
95 percent confidence interval using a design factor of 1.5. Dotted
line: the 1997--2024 mean (8.6). The gray band marks the Covid
supplements. The occupation classification changes between calendar
2001 and 2002 (1990 to 2000 Census codes); a core-codes-only series
shows the same profile (Appendix~\ref{app:figures}).
\textit{Source:} Author's calculations from March CPS supplements,
survey years 1998--2025.}
\end{figure}

Three features deserve emphasis. First, the level: 8.6 percent on
average, never below 7, above 10 only three times in twenty-eight
years. Second, there is no trend: the two highest points sit eighteen
years apart, one at the end of a boom and one on the eve of a
pandemic, and the series ends below its mean. The Covid episode reads
as an excursion, not a break---10.2 in 2019, 9.0 in 2020, 7.1 in 2021,
back to trend since. Third, the stability suggests a particular
framing. An annual outflow of eight percent, composed
overwhelmingly---as the next section shows---of retirements, family
spells, and early-career sorting, is best understood as the
profession's natural rate of attrition: the turnover a
five-million-person, majority-female, pension-covered occupation
generates in the ordinary course of life-cycle events. Fluctuations
around it have never exceeded two points in either direction. Any
policy narrative that begins from an accelerating exodus begins from a
fact the data do not contain.

\section{Exit Routes}\label{sec:routes}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/g3_flow100.pdf}
\caption{Destinations of Teachers Who Leave the Profession}
\label{fig:flow100}
\fignote{\textit{Notes:} College-graduate leavers pooled over calendar
2015--2024, ASEC weights. Top bar: labor force status the March after
the teaching year; ``out of the labor force'' is split at age 55.
Bottom bar: the occupational destination of the 26 employed leavers,
rescaled to sum to their total. \textit{Source:} Author's calculations
from March CPS supplements.}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:flow100} decomposes the flow. Of every 100
college-graduate teachers who leave, 62 are not working the following
March: 38 are out of the labor force at ages 55 or older---the
retirement route---24 are out of the labor force younger than 55, and
12 are unemployed. Only 26 hold a job. And of those 26, eight remain
in education or care work---school administration, counseling,
childcare---so that barely one leaver in six matches the scenario the
exodus narrative describes: a working-age teacher productively
employed outside education a year later.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/g4_routes_age.pdf}
\caption{Leaving Teaching by Age and Route}
\label{fig:routesage}
\fignote{\textit{Notes:} College-graduate teachers pooled over
calendar 2015--2024, ASEC weights; ten age groups. The black line is
the total leaving rate; stacked areas decompose it into exits to
another job (gold), unemployment (gray), and out of the labor force
(blue). \textit{Source:} Author's calculations from March CPS
supplements.}
\end{figure}

The age profile in Figure~\ref{fig:routesage} shows where each route
lives. Total leaving traces the classic U: 11 percent among teachers
aged 21--25, a flat 4--5 percent through ages 41--55, then 9, 17, and
28 percent in the three oldest groups. The decomposition explains both
arms. The left arm is career search: nearly half of the exit of the
youngest teachers is to another job, a share that decays rapidly with
age---and, notably, the early hump is a male phenomenon (15 percent
for men aged 21--25 versus 9 for women). The right arm is retirement,
almost entirely: from age 55 the out-of-labor-force route accounts for
nine tenths of a rapidly rising total, while the job-switching route
is flat at 1.5 percent from age 35 to the end. Unemployment never
exceeds two percent at any age. Mid-career teachers---the group
schools most need to retain, and the group the crisis narrative
implicitly describes---are the least mobile workers in the figure.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.62\linewidth]{figures/g10_sector_routes.pdf}
\caption{Exit Routes by School Sector}
\label{fig:sector}
\fignote{\textit{Notes:} Route-specific annual exit rates of
college-graduate teachers by sector of the last-year job
(class-of-worker: government vs.\ private), pooled 2015--2024, ASEC
weights. A private-school teacher who moves to a public school---or
vice versa---remains a teacher and is not a leaver here.
\textit{Source:} Author's calculations from March CPS supplements.}
\end{figure}

One split deserves its own figure. Private school teachers leave the
profession at nearly twice the public rate---11.6 versus 6.8 percent a
year---and the excess runs through every route
(Figure~\ref{fig:sector}): to other jobs (3.7 vs.\ 1.4), to
unemployment (1.5 vs.\ 0.8), and out of the labor force (6.4 vs.\
4.6). Because sector switching within teaching does not count as
leaving, this is genuine profession attrition, not churn between
sectors. The gap is persistent across the whole window and
concentrated among the young: a private school loses roughly one in
five of its under-30 teachers to the profession every year. The public
sector premium in retention---tenure protections, salary schedules,
and above all pension coverage---previews the multivariate result of
Section~\ref{sec:probit}.

\section{The Family Margin}
\label{sec:family}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.82\linewidth]{figures/g8_newbaby.pdf}
\caption{The Effect of a New Baby on Leaving, by Profession}
\label{fig:newbaby}
\fignote{\textit{Notes:} Average marginal effect of a new baby (own
child aged 0 in the household) on the probability that a woman leaves
each profession, from profession-specific probits with age, age
squared, marital status, education, and year effects; whiskers are 95
percent confidence intervals. Solid bars are significant at 5 percent;
light bars are not. Samples pool 2010--2024. \textit{Source:} Author's
calculations from March CPS supplements.}
\end{figure}

If the contract is the main systematic predictor of exit, the main
personal event is a birth. In the pooled model of
Section~\ref{sec:probit}, a new baby raises a teacher's probability of
leaving by 4.7 percentage points; among women the effect is 5.9
points. Figure~\ref{fig:newbaby} puts that number in comparative
perspective: the teaching effect is roughly double the birth-year exit
in nursing (2.6) and accounting (3.5), the two comparison professions
where the effect is also statistically detectable.

Why teaching? Two readings coexist, and the data support both. The
harsher one is rigidity: teaching offers no part-time ladder, no shift
swaps, and no remote option---a teacher either covers her classroom at
its hours or does not work---so the arrival of an infant forces a
binary choice that nursing, with its shift structure, does not force.
The gentler one runs the logic of career interruption costs in
\citet{goldin2014} in reverse: teaching is the college profession
where stepping out is cheapest---certification persists, seniority
premia are flat, jobs exist everywhere---so mothers who in another
career would hang on through the infant years can afford to pause. A
linked-sample audit supports the second reading as a substantial
component: about a third of new-mother leavers are observed teaching
again within a year. The birth effect, in other words, is partly a
revolving door, not only an exit.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{figures/g9_mothers.pdf}
\caption{Labor Force Exit of Women Teachers, With and Without Young
Children}
\label{fig:mothers}
\fignote{\textit{Notes:} Women teachers aged 22--45, college
graduates; annual probability of leaving the labor force (thin lines)
and centered 3-year averages (bold), separately for those with and
without an own child under 6 at home. Endpoint values printed.
\textit{Source:} Author's calculations from March CPS supplements,
survey years 1998--2025.}
\end{figure}

Whatever its interpretation in the cross-section, the margin is
disappearing in the time series. Figure~\ref{fig:mothers} tracks the
probability that a young woman teacher leaves the labor force,
separately for mothers of children under six and for other women. In
the late 1990s the gap was five to seven points a year---the classic
exit to home. It has narrowed almost continuously for twenty-five
years and stands below two points at the end of the window (4.7 versus
3.0 in 2024). Combined with the falling share of teachers who have
young children at all (Figure~\ref{fig:workforce}), the family route
now explains a shrinking slice of total attrition---the composition
shift visible in Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:routesevol}. A state-level
test of whether paid family leave programs accelerate this decline is
underpowered but points the right way: the birth-year exit effect is
2.1 points smaller in state-years with an active program, on only 268
treated births (Appendix~\ref{app:tables}, Table~\ref{tab:pfl}).

\section{Attrition and the Business Cycle}\label{sec:cycle}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/n5_cyclicality.pdf}
\caption{Leaving Teaching and the Unemployment Rate, 1997--2024}
\label{fig:cycle}
\fignote{\textit{Notes:} Left: the leaving series of
Figure~\ref{fig:evolution} (annual, from calendar 2000) against the
BLS national unemployment rate (annual average). Right: binned scatter
(deciles of the unemployment rate in the survey month, March of
$t{+}1$, when the exit is observed) of the two labor-market routes,
with OLS fits on the underlying annual data; correlations are $-0.16$
(to another job) and $+0.29$ (out of the labor force). Unemployment:
BLS via FRED (\texttt{UNRATE}). \textit{Source:} Author's calculations
from March CPS supplements.}
\end{figure}

A popular version of the exodus story is cyclical: when the economy
booms, outside options improve and teachers are poached. The data say
close to the opposite. In levels (Figure~\ref{fig:cycle}, left),
leaving is a flat line while unemployment swings through three
recessions; the two series touch only around Covid. In the
cross-section of years (right panel), the job-switching route is
essentially acyclical---if anything it falls when unemployment is high
($r=-0.16$), as fewer outside offers arrive---while the withdrawal
route rises moderately in downturns ($r=+0.29$): slack markets bring
forward retirements and returns to home production, an old pattern in
female labor supply. The route into unemployment correlates $+0.55$
with the aggregate rate, a near-mechanical validation of the
instrument.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{figures/n5b_states_trio.pdf}
\caption{Leaving and State Unemployment: Two Examples}
\label{fig:states}
\fignote{\textit{Notes:} Annual state leaving rates of
college-graduate teachers (dots; one per year, 1997--2024) against the
state unemployment rate in the survey month (BLS Local Area
Unemployment Statistics), with OLS fits. North Carolina and New Jersey
are shown as examples of the two signs; the full 51-state distribution
of slopes is in Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:stateforest}. State of
residence is recovered from the household records of the raw files
(Appendix~\ref{app:construction}). \textit{Source:} Author's
calculations from March CPS supplements.}
\end{figure}

State-level variation sharpens the asymmetry. Estimating the
within-state relationship between leaving and the state unemployment
rate for all fifty-one jurisdictions over twenty-eight years yields a
distribution of slopes centered near zero---in the large states, where
power is real, the estimates are tight zeros (California $+0.21\pm
0.22$)---with a telling tail: the only four states where the
relationship is statistically distinguishable from zero (Mississippi,
Arizona, Maine, Indiana) all lie on the ``exits rise in downturns''
side, and not one state shows a significant ``exits rise in booms''
pattern (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:stateforest}).
Figure~\ref{fig:states} illustrates the two signs with one state each.
Where teacher exits respond to the cycle at all, recessions push;
booms do not pull.

\section{Teacher Pay and the Returns to Leaving}
\label{sec:pay}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/g5b_earnings_density.pdf}
\caption{The Distribution of Earnings Changes: Stayers and Leavers}
\label{fig:density}
\fignote{\textit{Notes:} Kernel densities of the person-level change
in annual wage and salary earnings (log points $\times$100) between
the teaching year and the next, for teachers linked across consecutive
supplements (rotation-group links, survey years 2011--2025; identity
validated on sex and age). Navy: stayers (12,195 links; median $+3$).
Red: leavers employed the following year (225 links; median $+15$).

\end{figure}

Does leaving pay? Linking teachers across consecutive Marches prices
each transition at the person level, and Figure~\ref{fig:density}
draws the result. Stayers experience ordinary wage growth: a median of
$+3$ log points, tightly distributed. Leavers who land in another job
show a median of $+15$---a real premium---but around that median the
distribution is far wider than the stayers': 24 percent lose more than
30 log points, 45 percent gain more than 30. And the figure conditions
on earning anything at all. Forty-seven percent of leavers report zero
wage and salary earnings the following year, consistent with the route
decomposition of Figure~\ref{fig:flow100}: most leavers were not
walking into better-paid jobs, because most leavers were not walking
into jobs. Leaving teaching for money is a gamble with a modest median
prize and substantial downside risk; for a risk-averse,
pension-covered workforce, the low and stable exit rate of
Figure~\ref{fig:evolution} is the expected response.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{figures/n6b_pay_windows.pdf}
\caption{Real Median Earnings by Profession, 2004--2014 and
2015--2025}
\label{fig:paywindows}
\fignote{\textit{Notes:} Weighted median annual wage and salary
earnings of full-time, full-year college graduates aged 25--60, in
each profession and overall, deflated to 2024 dollars with the CPI-U;

March CPS supplements; CPI: BLS via FRED.}
\end{figure}

The staying side of the ledger has deteriorated. In real terms, the
median full-time, full-year teacher earns \$66{,}000 (2024 dollars) in
the recent decade---essentially unchanged from the decade
before---while registered nurses moved to \$92{,}000, accountants to
\$89{,}000, and the median college graduate to \$90{,}000
(Figure~\ref{fig:paywindows}). Indexed to 2002, teacher real pay ends
the window six percent below its starting point, against $+3$ for
nurses and $+8$ for accountants (Appendix
Figure~\ref{fig:payindex}); as a ratio, teacher pay slid from 80 to 72
percent of the median college graduate. This is the CPS version of the
teacher pay penalty documented by \citet{allegretto2023}, measured
here with one consistent instrument alongside everything else in the
paper. The juxtaposition with Section~\ref{sec:professions} is the
point: the profession whose relative pay fell furthest is also the
only one whose attrition declined. Pay operates on margins the exit
rate hides---who enters, who feels trapped, how retention interacts
with pensions---not as a simple valve on the outflow.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figures/g6_ame.pdf}
\caption{Average Marginal Effects on the Probability of Leaving
Teaching}
\label{fig:ame}
\fignote{\textit{Notes:} Average marginal effects (percentage points)
on the probability of leaving teaching, from a probit on

\textit{Source:} Author's calculations from March CPS supplements.}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:ame} assembles the portrait in one model, and the
dominant predictors are contractual, not personal. A teacher who
worked the full year is 9.3 points less likely to leave; part-time
hours add 4.5 points; pension coverage subtracts 2.4; a public school
subtracts 1.7. Against these, demographics are small: each year of age
subtracts 0.7 (the U-shape enters through age squared), women are 1.4
points less likely to leave than men, Black teachers 1.9 more, a
master's degree subtracts 0.9. The family block reduces to a single
variable: the number of children and the presence of a child under six
do essentially nothing, but the birth year itself adds 4.7 points---the
event, not the state, drives the exit.

The appendix battery shows the portrait is not an artifact of
specification. Across probit, weighted LPM, logit, state-and-year
fixed effects, the full 1997--2024 window ($n=85{,}430$), and the
occupation-pair definition of leaving, the job-attachment and birth
coefficients never lose significance or change sign
(Table~\ref{tab:robustness}). The subsample estimates
(Table~\ref{tab:subsamples}) add two further findings: a child under
six raises women's exit ($+1.3$) but lowers men's ($-2.2$), a pattern
consistent with household specialization; and pension coverage retains
men more than twice as strongly as women ($-4.5$ versus $-2.0$).

\section{Policy Implications}\label{sec:policy}

This paper set out to measure, not to prescribe; but a measurement
this long constrains prescriptions, and it is worth stating how.

\textbf{Retire the exodus frame.} Profession leaving is flat at its
generation-long level, was flat through Covid within two years, and is
lower today than in 2001. Policies premised on an accelerating
outflow---emergency certification pipelines, across-the-board
retention bonuses---address a trend that does not exist, and their
evaluation against a mythical baseline will always look like success.
The turnover problem American schools do have is distributional---by
school, subject, and district
\citep{carverthomas2017,hanushek2004}---and the CPS, which sees
professions rather than schools, is the wrong lens for it; but a
national policy sold on the national rate is answerable to the
national rate.

\textbf{Aim at the margins that move.} The anatomy locates three. The
retirement route (38 of every 100 leavers) responds to pension rules,
and its mean age has already drifted from 61.3 to 65.2 across the
window---policies that manage the timing of exits (phased retirement,
return-to-work rules) operate on the largest single flow. The family
route responds to the compatibility of teaching with an infant at
home: the birth-year exit is twice nursing's, a third of it is a
revolving door already, and the fading of the motherhood exit shows
the margin is elastic to social change; job-protected paid leave and
genuine part-time ladders are the obvious levers, and the underpowered
but right-signed paid-leave interaction estimated here suggests the
experiment is worth running properly. The early-career route (11
percent at ages 21--25, half of it to other jobs, disproportionately
male and private-sector) is standard career search; the productive
response is selection and early support, not universal retention
spending on a flow that mostly self-corrects by age 30.

\textbf{Read the pay problem correctly.} Nothing here says pay does
not matter; the point is where it matters. The exit rate cannot fall
much---it is already anchored by routes pay does not touch---so the
return to raising relative pay lies elsewhere: in who chooses to
enter, in the quality margin of who stays, and in fairness to a
workforce whose real median earnings have not grown in twenty years
while every comparison profession's did. The pension result carries a
specific warning: coverage retains (2.4 points, twice that for men),
and coverage has fallen 14 points in two decades. Letting the deferred
part of the compensation package erode while debating the current part
is, on this evidence, exactly backwards.

\textbf{Treat teaching's turnover as the price of its accessibility.}
The comparison figures reframe the profession's churn: teaching pays
72 cents on the college dollar, yet loses fewer of its members than
social work, therapy, or (now) pharmacy, and uniquely improved across
decades. Part of the reason is the same flexibility that shows up as
the birth-year spike and the revolving door: teaching is cheap to
leave and cheap to re-enter, which makes it the college profession
most compatible with an interrupted life. A policy agenda that treated
that accessibility as an asset to be organized---formal re-entry
paths, leave that preserves attachment, part-time roles that are
careers rather than exits---would work with the grain of the anatomy
documented here.

The contribution of this paper is the anatomy itself: one instrument,
twenty-eight consecutive years, every exit routed, priced, and
modeled. On that evidence, the American teaching profession is not
hemorrhaging. It is aging, underpaid relative to its peers, uniquely
exposed to the arithmetic of family life, and steady---losing its
members to the same life events it has always lost them to, at the
same rate, while the conversation about it swings with each news
cycle. Policy should be built on the steadiness, not the swings.

\section{Instrument Validation}\label{app:instrument}

For respondents in CPS rotation groups that yield four consecutive
monthly interviews after the reference year together with a March
retrospective response, profession leaving can be classified two
independent ways: by the within-panel criterion (teaching occupation in

short, reproduces at the person level the classification a
