\begin{center}\bfseries Abstract\end{center}\vspace{2pt}
Every few years the United States rediscovers that teachers quit, and
calls it an exodus. This paper builds the longest and deepest anatomy of
teacher attrition available from a single instrument: the March Current
Population Survey question comparing last year's longest job with this
week's occupation, read from the raw Census files at their officially
documented positions for twenty-eight consecutive supplements, calendar
1997--2024. The rate of leaving the profession has no trend: it averages
8.6 percent over twenty-eight years, peaked at 10.3 in 2001 and 10.2 in
2019, and stood at 7.7 in 2024. Its anatomy explains why the number is
so stable and so misread. Of every 100 leavers, 62 exit the labor force
altogether---38 at retirement ages---12 are unemployed the following
March, and only 26 hold another job, a third of them still in education
and care. Exits barely respond to the business cycle, and where they do,
downturns push teachers out; booms do not pull them away. For the
minority who do switch careers, the move is a lottery rather than a
raise: the median switcher gains 15 log points, but a quarter lose more
than 30 and nearly half of all leavers report no earnings at all the
next year. What predicts exit is the contract, not the person:
part-year work, part-time hours, and missing pension coverage dwarf
every demographic; the one personal event that matters is a birth,
which raises exit by five points---an effect that is twice as large as
in nursing and that has been fading for two decades as the motherhood
exit disappears. Meanwhile teaching is the only comparison profession
whose attrition \emph{fell} between 2004--2014 and 2015--2025, even as
its relative pay slid from 80 to 72 percent of the median college
graduate. Teaching does not lose more people than comparable
professions; it loses them differently---to retirement systems and to
family---and policy aimed at a generic exodus misses both margins.}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

There is a real tension at the heart of the American conversation about
teachers. On one side, the concern is legitimate and well documented:
turnover is costly for schools and students
\citep{ronfeldt2013,hanushek2004}, staffing is chronically hard in
special education, mathematics, and high-poverty schools
\citep{carverthomas2017}, and teacher pay has fallen visibly behind
other college-graduate careers \citep{allegretto2023}. On the other
side, the flagship statistic of the alarm---``one in six teachers walks
away every year''---does not survive contact with its own sources. The
16 percent figure combines two very different behaviors: teachers who
move between schools but keep teaching, and teachers who actually leave
the profession. The second group, the one the word ``exodus'' evokes,
has been seven to eight percent of the workforce for as long as the data
can see, in every dataset that measures it.

This paper documents that fact and everything underneath it, using one
instrument pushed as far as it will go. The March Current Population
Survey (CPS) asks every household about the \emph{longest job held last
calendar year} and about the \emph{occupation this week}. Whoever taught
last year and no longer teaches this March has left teaching within a
window of at most fifteen months---and the CPS then reports where they
landed: another occupation, unemployment, or out of the labor force,
together with their earnings before and after. Harris and Adams
(\citeyear{harris2007}) introduced this design; Aldeman and Yi
(\citeyear{aldeman2025}) recently used its modern files to make the
level; the follow-up surveys of the National Center for Education
Statistics (NCES) confirm the same level from school rosters
\citep{nces2024}. What no one has done is run the instrument at full
depth and full length: every supplement from 1998 to 2025, read from
the raw fixed-width Census files at the byte positions of the official
technical documentation, harmonized into a single person-level database
of five million records, validated person-by-person against
within-panel classifications, decomposed by route, age, sex, sector,
state, and family event, priced by linking consecutive Marches, and
modeled with the covariates the ASEC carries. That is the contribution:
not a new theory of teacher labor markets, but the longest and most
complete measurement of one, with temporal comparison built into every
figure.

Five findings organize the paper. \emph{First, the level is flat and
always has been.} Leaving teaching averaged 8.6 percent over
1997--2024, with two nearly identical peaks a generation apart (10.3
in 2001, 10.2 in 2019) and no trend in between or since
(Figure~\ref{fig:evolution}). Covid produced a two-year excursion that
fully reversed. \emph{Second, most leaving is not career change.} Of
every 100 leavers, 62 exit the labor force---38 of them at ages 55 or
older---and only 26 work in another job the following March
(Figure~\ref{fig:flow100}). The age profile is the classic U:\ 11
percent of the youngest teachers leave, 4 percent at mid-career, 28
percent at 65--80, and the retirement route accounts for essentially
the entire right arm (Figure~\ref{fig:routesage}). \emph{Third, the
family margin is large, specific, and dying.} A new baby raises a
woman's probability of leaving teaching by 5.9 percentage points,
roughly double the effect in nursing and accounting
(Figure~\ref{fig:newbaby}); but the exit-to-home route of young
mothers has collapsed from 8--10 percent a year in the late 1990s to
under 5 today (Figure~\ref{fig:mothers}), and the family share of all
exits has shrunk accordingly. \emph{Fourth, exits are weakly
counter-cyclical, not pro-cyclical.} Across twenty-eight years the
job-switching route is essentially acyclical ($r=-0.16$ against the
unemployment rate at observation), while the withdrawal routes rise
mildly in downturns ($r=+0.29$); across states, every statistically
detectable case is of the ``recessions push out'' variety and none of
the ``booms pull away'' variety (Figures~\ref{fig:cycle}
and~\ref{fig:states}). The story in which a hot labor market lures
teachers into better-paid jobs does not appear in the data. \emph{Fifth,
pay explains retention patterns better than exit levels.} Leaving for
another job is a gamble---median gain 15 log points, fat tails on both
sides, 47 percent of all leavers with zero earnings the next year
(Figures~\ref{fig:gamble} and~\ref{fig:density})---which is exactly why
so few take it. Meanwhile teacher pay fell from 80 to 72 percent of the
median college graduate over the window, and teaching is nonetheless
the only comparison profession whose attrition \emph{declined} between
the two halves of the sample (Figures~\ref{fig:paywindows}
and~\ref{fig:professions}).

The paper is organized around its figures. Section~\ref{sec:data}
describes the instrument, the pooled database, the definitions that
reconcile the numbers in the public debate, and the validation
exercises. Sections~\ref{sec:workforce}--\ref{sec:professions} present
the anatomy: the changing workforce, the rate, the routes, the family
margin, the cycle, pay, the multivariate portrait, and the comparison
professions. Section~\ref{sec:policy} concludes with what the anatomy
implies for policy. Appendices carry the instrument audit, the
comparison with \citet{harris2007}, construction details, robustness
tables, and additional figures.

\section{Data and measurement}\label{sec:data}

\subsection{The March retrospective instrument}

The Annual Social and Economic Supplement (ASEC) of the CPS, fielded
every March, asks two questions that together identify profession
leaving. The first is retrospective: the occupation of the
\emph{longest job held during the previous calendar year}
(\texttt{OCCUP}). The second is contemporaneous: the occupation of the
job held \emph{this week} (\texttt{PEIOOCC}), together with the labor
force status recode (\texttt{A\_LFSR}), which distinguishes the
employed, the unemployed, and those outside the labor force. A
\emph{teacher} is a respondent whose longest job last year carried a
K--12 teaching occupation code (preschool through secondary and special
education, plus the ``other teachers'' residual; postsecondary
teachers, aides, and tutors are excluded). A \emph{leaver} is a teacher
who, by the March interview, is not employed in a teaching occupation:
either working in some other occupation (\emph{switchers}), unemployed,
or out of the labor force. Because status comes from \texttt{A\_LFSR}
rather than from the occupation variable---which carries the last job
held even for the unemployed---the three routes are mutually exclusive
and exhaustive.

Three features of the design matter for interpretation and recur
throughout the paper. \emph{Timing:} the supplement of survey year
$t{+}1$ measures calendar year $t$; the exit itself occurs at some
point between the end of the teaching spell in year $t$ and the March
interview---for teachers, typically at the end of a school year. We
therefore date exits by the calendar year taught and, where the labor
market at the moment of observation matters
(Section~\ref{sec:cycle}), measure it in the survey month itself.
\emph{Leave is not exit:} a teacher on maternity or medical leave in
March reports ``with a job, not at work,'' counts as employed, and---if
the job is a teaching job---as a stayer. Our leavers are genuine
separations. \emph{Movers are invisible by design:} a teacher who
changes school but keeps teaching keeps the occupation code and is a
stayer. This is precisely what makes the CPS number a measure of
\emph{profession} attrition rather than school turnover.

\subsection{One pooled database from the raw files}

All results come from a single person-level database built from the
original Census public-use files: 5,009,129 person-year records across
the 28 supplements of survey years 1998--2025, containing 104,545
teacher-year observations (85,497 for college graduates aged 18 and
over, the baseline universe). For survey years 2011--2025 the modern
machine-readable files are used directly. For survey years 1998--2010
the public files are fixed-width records whose layouts are not
machine-readable; every variable was extracted at the byte positions
published in the official Census technical documentation
(\texttt{cpsmar98.pdf}--\texttt{cpsmar10.pdf}), so that education,
supplement weights, labor force status, demographics, household
structure, last-year job characteristics (weeks, hours, class of
worker, pension coverage), earnings, and state of residence exist on a
common footing in \emph{every} year. Occupation codes are harmonized
across the four Census classification regimes with era-consistent code
sets. Appendix~\ref{app:construction} documents the construction;
Table~\ref{tab:sources} reports record counts by source. All series are
weighted with the ASEC supplement weight unless noted.

\subsection{What ``leaving'' means: reconciling the public numbers}
\label{sec:definitions}

The headline numbers in the policy debate are consistent with one
another once definitions are aligned; the alarm comes from mixing them.
The NCES Teacher Follow-up Survey reports \emph{total turnover} of
public school teachers of 13--16 percent across its waves. That total
is the sum of two components the survey itself keeps separate:
\emph{movers}, who change school but keep teaching (5--8 percent per
wave), and \emph{leavers}, who exit the profession (5.6 percent in
1988--89, rising to 7.4--8.4 percent from 2000--01 onward)
\citep{nces2024,tan2025}. Aldeman and Yi's CPS estimate of roughly 7.6
percent measures leavers only \citep{aldeman2025}, as does Harris and
Adams's 7.7 percent for 1992--2001 \citep{harris2007}, and as does our
series, which averages 8.2 percent for college-graduate teachers over
2015--2024 and 8.6 percent over the full window. There is no
contradiction anywhere in this literature: school-to-school mobility
roughly doubles profession leaving, and only the latter belongs in a
sentence containing the word ``exodus.'' Our estimates sit slightly
above the NCES leaver rates for overlapping years, as they should: the
CPS covers private school teachers, whose attrition is nearly twice
the public rate (Section~\ref{sec:routes}), while the NCES figure is
public-only.

\subsection{Validation}\label{sec:validation}

Two audits back the instrument. First, a person-level cross-validation:
for respondents observed in the CPS monthly rotation long enough to be
classified by a four-month within-panel criterion \emph{and} answering
the March retrospective question, the two classifications agree in 99.1
percent of cases ($n=9{,}461$;
Appendix~\ref{app:instrument}, Table~\ref{tab:confusion}). Second, a
timing audit: the correlation of the leaving series with the national
unemployment rate is $+0.08$ using the year \emph{before} the teaching
year, $+0.32$ using the teaching year itself, and $+0.47$ using the
year the exit is observed---exactly the gradient the retrospective
design implies, and evidence that the instrument dates transitions
where it claims to. External anchors point the same way: the NCES
leaver rates for its five overlapping waves fall within a point of our
series.

\section{A changing profession}\label{sec:workforce}

Figure~\ref{fig:workforce} tracks the composition of the teaching
workforce over the last two decades, panel by panel; each panel shows
the annual series in light gray and a three-year average in blue. Four
slow-moving facts stand out, and each returns later in the paper as a
mechanical driver of attrition.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/g1_workforce.pdf}
\caption{The changing profile of the teaching workforce, 2005--2024.}
\label{fig:workforce}
\fignote{College-graduate teachers aged 18+ whose longest job last year
was K--12 teaching; ASEC supplement weights. Thin gray lines are annual
values; blue lines are centered 3-year averages, with endpoint values
printed. ``Public school,'' ``part-time,'' and ``pension plan'' refer
to the longest job held last year; the open circle marks 2013, a year
with a reduced sample due to the ASEC redesign. Source: author's
calculations from March CPS supplements, survey years 2006--2025.}
\end{figure}

The workforce is aging: mean age rose from 42.8 to 44.8, and the share
of teachers aged 55 or more rose with it---which matters because
Section~\ref{sec:routes} shows the 55+ retirement route is the largest
single component of attrition. It is increasingly credentialed (45 to
51 percent with a master's degree or more) and slowly diversifying (8
to 11 percent Black). The share with a young child at home has fallen
from 19 to 14 percent, the demographic echo of later and lower
fertility---and, as Section~\ref{sec:family} shows, the family exit
route has fallen even faster than the share of potential mothers.
Finally, the employment contract has deteriorated on the one margin
the attrition model will single out: pension coverage at the last-year
job fell from 80 to 66 percent, the steepest change in any panel.

\section{The rate: twenty-eight years without a trend}
\label{sec:evolution}

Figure~\ref{fig:evolution} is the paper's spine: the share of
college-graduate teachers who left the profession each calendar year
from 1997 to 2024, on one continuous, consistently defined, weighted
series---to our knowledge the longest ever assembled from this
instrument.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{figures/g2_evolution.pdf}
\caption{Leaving teaching, 1997--2024.}
\label{fig:evolution}
\fignote{Share of college-graduate teachers (longest job last calendar
year) not employed in a teaching occupation the following March, by
route-based definition; ASEC weights. Shaded band: 95 percent
confidence interval using a design factor of 1.5. Dotted line: the
1997--2024 mean (8.6). The gray band marks the Covid supplements. The
occupation classification changes between calendar 2001 and 2002
(1990 to 2000 Census codes); a core-codes-only series shows the same
profile (Appendix~\ref{app:figures}). Source: author's calculations
from March CPS supplements, survey years 1998--2025.}
\end{figure}

Three features deserve emphasis. First, the \emph{level}: 8.6 percent
on average, never below 7 and above 10 only three times in
twenty-eight years. Second, the \emph{absence of trend}: the two
highest points sit eighteen years apart---10.3 in 2001, at the end of
the dot-com boom, and 10.2 in 2019, on the eve of the pandemic---and
the series ends at 7.7, below its long-run mean. The Covid episode
reads as an excursion, not a break: 10.2 in 2019, 9.0 in 2020, 7.1 in
2021, back to trend since. Third, the \emph{framing} this stability
invites. An annual outflow of eight percent, composed
overwhelmingly---as the next section shows---of retirements, family
spells, and early-career sorting, is best understood as the
profession's \emph{natural rate} of attrition: the turnover a
five-million-person, majority-female, pension-covered occupation
generates in the ordinary course of life-cycle events. Fluctuations
around it have never exceeded two points in either direction. Any
policy narrative that begins from an accelerating exodus begins from a
fact the data do not contain.

\section{Where leavers go}\label{sec:routes}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/g3_flow100.pdf}
\caption{Of every 100 teachers who leave the profession.}
\label{fig:flow100}
\fignote{College-graduate leavers pooled over calendar 2015--2024,
ASEC weights. Top bar: labor force status the March after the teaching
year; ``out of the labor force'' is split at age 55. Bottom bar: the
occupational destination of the 26 employed leavers, rescaled to sum
to their total. Source: author's calculations from March CPS
supplements.}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:flow100} decomposes the flow. Of every 100
college-graduate teachers who leave, 62 are not working the following
March: 38 are out of the labor force at ages 55 or older---the
retirement route---24 are out of the labor force younger than 55, and
12 are unemployed. Only 26 hold a job. And of those 26, eight remain
in education or care work---school administration, counseling,
childcare---so that barely one leaver in six represents the scenario
the exodus narrative describes: a working-age teacher productively
employed outside education a year later.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/g4_routes_age.pdf}
\caption{Leaving by age, decomposed by route.}
\label{fig:routesage}
\fignote{College-graduate teachers pooled over calendar 2015--2024,
ASEC weights; ten age groups. The black line is the total leaving
rate; stacked areas decompose it into exits to another job (gold),
unemployment (gray), and out of the labor force (blue). Source:
author's calculations from March CPS supplements.}
\end{figure}

The age profile in Figure~\ref{fig:routesage} shows where each route
lives. Total leaving traces the classic U:\ 11 percent among teachers
aged 21--25, a flat 4--5 percent through ages 41--55, then 9, 17, and
28 percent in the three oldest groups. The decomposition explains both
arms. The left arm is career search: nearly half of the exit of the
youngest teachers is to another job, a share that decays rapidly with
age---and, notably, the early hump is a male phenomenon (15 percent
for men aged 21--25 versus 9 for women). The right arm is retirement,
almost entirely: from age 55 the out-of-labor-force route accounts for
nine tenths of a rapidly rising total, while the job-switching route
is flat at 1.5 percent from age 35 to the end. Unemployment never
exceeds two percent at any age. Mid-career teachers---the group
schools most need to retain and the group the crisis narrative
implicitly describes---are the least mobile workers in the figure.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.62\linewidth]{figures/g10_sector_routes.pdf}
\caption{Public and private school teachers leave differently.}
\label{fig:sector}
\fignote{Route-specific annual exit rates of college-graduate teachers
by sector of the last-year job (class-of-worker: government vs.\
private), pooled 2015--2024, ASEC weights. A private-school teacher
who moves to a public school---or vice versa---remains a teacher and
is not a leaver here. Source: author's calculations from March CPS
supplements.}
\end{figure}

One split deserves its own figure. Private school teachers leave the
profession at nearly twice the public rate---11.6 versus 6.8 percent a
year---and the excess runs through \emph{every} route
(Figure~\ref{fig:sector}): to other jobs (3.7 vs.\ 1.4), to
unemployment (1.5 vs.\ 0.8), and out of the labor force (6.4 vs.\
4.6). Because sector switching within teaching does not count as
leaving, this is genuine profession attrition, not churn between
sectors. The gap is persistent across the whole window and
concentrated among the young: a private school loses roughly one in
five of its under-30 teachers to the profession every year. The public
sector premium in retention---tenure protections, salary schedules,
and above all pension coverage---previews the multivariate result of
Section~\ref{sec:probit}.

\section{The family margin, and its slow disappearance}
\label{sec:family}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.82\linewidth]{figures/g8_newbaby.pdf}
\caption{The birth-year exit, teaching versus seven professions.}
\label{fig:newbaby}
\fignote{Average marginal effect of a new baby (own child aged 0 in the
household) on the probability that a woman leaves each profession, from
profession-specific probits with age, age squared, marital status,
education, and year effects; whiskers are 95 percent confidence
intervals. Solid bars are significant at 5 percent; light bars are
not. Samples pool 2010--2024. Source: author's calculations from March
CPS supplements.}
\end{figure}

If the contract is the main systematic predictor of exit, the main
personal event is a birth. In the pooled model of
Section~\ref{sec:probit}, a new baby raises a teacher's probability of
leaving by 4.7 percentage points; among women the effect is 5.9
points. Figure~\ref{fig:newbaby} puts that number in comparative
perspective: the teaching effect is roughly double the birth-year exit
in nursing (2.6) and accounting (3.5), the two comparison professions
where the effect is also statistically detectable.

Two readings of this gap coexist, and the data support both. The
harsher one is rigidity: teaching offers no part-time ladder, no shift
swaps, and no remote option---a teacher either covers her classroom at
its hours or does not work---so the arrival of an infant forces a
binary choice that nursing, with its shift structure, does not force.
The gentler one is Goldin's (\citeyear{goldin2014}) logic of career
interruption costs run in reverse: teaching is the college profession
where stepping out is \emph{cheapest}---certification persists,
seniority premia are flat, jobs exist everywhere---so mothers who in
another career would hang on through the infant years can afford to
pause. A linked-sample audit supports the second reading as a
substantial component: about a third of new-mother leavers are
observed teaching again within a year. The birth effect, in other
words, is partly a revolving door, not only an exit.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{figures/g9_mothers.pdf}
\caption{The fading motherhood exit, 1997--2024.}
\label{fig:mothers}
\fignote{Women teachers aged 22--45, college graduates; annual
probability of leaving the labor force (thin lines) and centered
3-year averages (bold), separately for those with and without an own
child under 6 at home. Endpoint values printed. Source: author's
calculations from March CPS supplements, survey years 1998--2025.}
\end{figure}

Whatever its interpretation in the cross-section, the margin is
disappearing in the time series. Figure~\ref{fig:mothers} tracks the
probability that a young woman teacher leaves the labor force,
separately for mothers of children under six and for other women. In
the late 1990s the gap was five to seven points a year---the classic
exit to home. It has narrowed almost continuously for twenty-five
years and stands below two points at the end of the window (4.7 versus
3.0 in 2024). Combined with the falling share of teachers who have
young children at all (Figure~\ref{fig:workforce}), the family route
explains a shrinking slice of total attrition---the composition shift
visible in Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:routesevol}. A state-level test of
whether paid family leave programs accelerate this decline is
underpowered but points the right way: the birth-year exit effect is
2.1 points smaller in state-years with an active program, on only 268
treated births (Appendix~\ref{app:tables}).

\section{Attrition and the business cycle}\label{sec:cycle}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/n5_cyclicality.pdf}
\caption{Leaving teaching and the labor market, 1997--2024.}
\label{fig:cycle}
\fignote{Left: the leaving series of Figure~\ref{fig:evolution}
(annual, from calendar 2000) against the BLS national unemployment
rate (annual average). Right: binned scatter (deciles of the
unemployment rate in the survey month, March of $t{+}1$, when the exit
is observed) of the two labor-market routes, with OLS fits on the
underlying annual data; correlations are $-0.16$ (to another job) and
$+0.29$ (out of the labor force). Unemployment: BLS via FRED
(\texttt{UNRATE}). Source: author's calculations from March CPS
supplements.}
\end{figure}

A popular version of the exodus story is cyclical: when the economy
booms, outside options improve and teachers are poached. The data say
close to the opposite. In levels (Figure~\ref{fig:cycle}, left),
leaving is a flat line while unemployment swings through three
recessions; the two series touch only around Covid. In the
cross-section of years (right panel), the job-switching route is
essentially acyclical---if anything it \emph{falls} when unemployment
is high ($r=-0.16$), as fewer outside offers arrive---while the
withdrawal route rises moderately in downturns ($r=+0.29$): slack
markets bring forward retirements and returns to home production, an
old pattern in female labor supply. The route into unemployment
correlates $+0.55$ with the aggregate rate, a near-mechanical
validation of the instrument.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{figures/n5b_states_trio.pdf}
\caption{Two illustrative states.}
\label{fig:states}
\fignote{Annual state leaving rates of college-graduate teachers
(dots; one per year, 1997--2024) against the state unemployment rate
in the survey month (BLS Local Area Unemployment Statistics), with OLS
fits. North Carolina and New Jersey are shown as \emph{examples} of
the two signs; the full 51-state distribution of slopes is in Appendix

State-level variation sharpens the asymmetry. Estimating the
within-state relationship between leaving and the state unemployment
rate for all fifty-one jurisdictions over twenty-eight years yields a
distribution of slopes centered near zero---in the large states, where
power is real, the estimates are tight zeros (California $+0.21\pm
0.22$)---with a telling tail: the only four states where the
relationship is statistically distinguishable from zero (Mississippi,
Arizona, Maine, Indiana) all lie on the ``exits rise in downturns''
side, and not one state shows a significant ``exits rise in booms''
pattern (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:stateforest}).
Figure~\ref{fig:states} illustrates the two signs with one state each.
Where teacher exits respond to the cycle at all, recessions push;
booms do not pull.

\section{Pay: the gamble of leaving and the slide of staying}
\label{sec:pay}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.88\linewidth]{figures/g5_earnings.pdf}
\caption{The earnings change of leaving, linked March to March.}
\label{fig:gamble}
\fignote{Person-level change in annual wage and salary earnings
between the teaching year and the following year, for teachers linked
across consecutive supplements (rotation-group links, survey years
2011--2025; identity validated on sex and age). Boxes: 25th--75th
percentiles (dark) and 10th--90th (light); bars mark medians.
``Leavers, all'' conditions on positive earnings in both years; 47
percent of all leavers report zero earnings the following year and are
excluded from the boxes. Source: author's calculations from March CPS
supplements.}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/g5b_earnings_density.pdf}
\caption{The same distributions, drawn.}
\label{fig:density}
\fignote{Kernel densities of the person-level earnings change (log
points $\times$100) for stayers and for leavers employed the following
year; dashed lines mark medians. ``Lose big''/``win big'' denote
changes beyond $\pm$30 log points. Densities are estimated on 12,195
stayer and 225 leaver links; bandwidth 0.25. Source: author's
calculations from March CPS supplements.}
\end{figure}

Does leaving pay? The honest answer is: it \emph{can}, and that is
different from \emph{does}. Linking teachers across consecutive
Marches prices each transition at the person level
(Figure~\ref{fig:gamble}). Stayers experience ordinary wage growth: a
median of $+3$ log points with a tight distribution. Leavers who land
in another job show a median of $+15$---but around it, dispersion
several times the stayers': the tenth percentile loses 85 log points,
the ninetieth gains 190. Figure~\ref{fig:density} draws the two
distributions: the leaver density is a flattened tent with mass far
into both tails---24 percent lose more than 30 log points, 45 percent
gain more than 30. And all of this conditions on earning anything at
all: 47 percent of leavers report zero wage and salary earnings the
following year, consistent with the route decomposition of
Figure~\ref{fig:flow100}. Leaving teaching for money is a lottery with
a modest median prize; the rational response of a risk-averse,
pension-covered workforce is to buy few tickets, and that is what
Figure~\ref{fig:evolution} shows.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{figures/n6b_pay_windows.pdf}
\caption{Real pay by profession, two symmetric windows.}
\label{fig:paywindows}
\fignote{Weighted median annual wage and salary earnings of full-time,
full-year college graduates aged 25--60, in each profession and
overall, deflated to 2024 dollars with the CPI-U; the two windows pool
survey years 2004--2014 and 2015--2025. Filled dots (with values):
recent window. Occupation codes harmonized across classification
regimes. Source: author's calculations from March CPS supplements;
CPI: BLS via FRED.}
\end{figure}

The staying side of the ledger has deteriorated. In real terms, the
median full-time, full-year teacher earns \$66{,}000 (2024 dollars) in
the recent decade---essentially unchanged from the decade
before---while registered nurses moved to \$92{,}000, accountants to
\$89{,}000, and the median college graduate to \$90{,}000
(Figure~\ref{fig:paywindows}). Indexed to 2002, teacher real pay ends
the window six percent \emph{below} its starting point, against $+3$
for nurses and $+8$ for accountants (Appendix
Figure~\ref{fig:payindex}); as a ratio, teacher pay slid from 80 to 72
percent of the median college graduate. This is the CPS version of the
teacher pay penalty documented by \citet{allegretto2023}, measured
here with one consistent instrument alongside everything else in the
paper. The juxtaposition with Section~\ref{sec:professions} is the
point: the profession whose relative pay fell furthest is also the
only one whose attrition declined. Pay operates on margins the exit
rate hides---who enters, who feels trapped, how retention interacts
with pensions---not as a simple valve on the outflow.

\section{Who leaves: the multivariate portrait}\label{sec:probit}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figures/g6_ame.pdf}
\caption{Who leaves teaching: probit average marginal effects.}
\label{fig:ame}
\fignote{Average marginal effects (percentage points) on the
probability of leaving teaching, from a probit on college-graduate
teachers pooled over calendar 2015--2024 with year effects; whiskers
are 95 percent confidence intervals; filled markers are significant at
5 percent. ``Worked full year'' is 50+ weeks at the last-year job;
``part-time'' is under 35 weekly hours. $n=28{,}900$. Robustness

and subsample estimates, are in Appendix~\ref{app:tables}. Source:
author's calculations from March CPS supplements.}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:ame} assembles the portrait in one model. The
dominant predictors are contractual. A teacher who worked the full
year is 9.3 points less likely to leave; part-time hours add 4.5
points; pension coverage subtracts 2.4; a public school subtracts 1.7.
Against these, demographics are small: each year of age subtracts 0.7
(the U-shape enters through age squared), women are 1.4 points less
likely to leave than men, Black teachers 1.9 more, a master's degree
subtracts 0.9. The family block is a tale of one variable: the number
of children and the presence of a child under six do essentially
nothing, but the \emph{birth year itself} adds 4.7 points---the event,
not the state, drives the exit.

The appendix battery shows the portrait is not an artifact of
specification. Across probit, weighted LPM, logit, state-and-year
fixed effects, the full 1997--2024 window ($n=85{,}430$), and the
occupation-pair definition of leaving, the job-attachment and birth
coefficients never lose significance or change sign
(Table~\ref{tab:robustness}). The subsample estimates
(Table~\ref{tab:subsamples}) add two findings worth text: a child
under six \emph{raises} women's exit (+1.3) but \emph{lowers}
men's ($-2.2$)---household specialization in one row---and pension
coverage retains men more than twice as strongly as women ($-4.5$
versus $-2.0$).

Is an eight percent outflow a lot? Figure~\ref{fig:professions}

\section{What the anatomy means for policy}\label{sec:policy}

This paper set out to measure, not to prescribe; but a measurement this
long constrains prescriptions, and it is worth stating how.

\emph{Retire the exodus frame.} Profession leaving is flat at its
generation-long level, was flat through Covid within two years, and is
lower today than in 2001. Policies premised on an accelerating outflow
---emergency certification pipelines, across-the-board retention
bonuses---address a trend that does not exist, and their evaluation
against a mythical baseline will always look like success. The
turnover problem American schools do have is distributional---by
school, subject, and district \citep{carverthomas2017,hanushek2004}---
and the CPS, which sees professions rather than schools, is the wrong
lens for it; but a national policy sold on the national rate is
answerable to the national rate.

\emph{Aim at the margins that move.} The anatomy locates three. The
retirement route (38 of every 100 leavers) responds to pension rules,
and its mean age has already drifted from 61.3 to 65.2 across the
window---policies that manage the timing of exits (phased retirement,
return-to-work rules) operate on the largest single flow. The family
route responds to the compatibility of teaching with an infant at
home: the birth-year exit is twice nursing's, a third of it is a
revolving door already, and the fading of the motherhood exit shows
the margin is elastic to social change; job-protected paid leave and
genuine part-time ladders are the obvious levers, and our underpowered
but right-signed paid-leave interaction suggests the experiment is
worth running properly. The early-career route (11 percent at ages
21--25, half of it to other jobs, disproportionately male and
private-sector) is standard career search; the productive response is
selection and early support, not universal retention spending on a
flow that mostly self-corrects by age 30.

\emph{Read the pay problem correctly.} Nothing here says pay does not
matter; the point is where it matters. The exit rate cannot fall much
---it is already anchored by routes pay does not touch---so the return
to raising relative pay lies elsewhere: in who chooses to enter, in
the quality margin of who stays, and in fairness to a workforce whose
real median earnings have not grown in twenty years while every
comparison profession's did. The pension result carries a specific
warning: coverage retains (2.4 points, twice that for men), and
coverage has fallen 14 points in two decades. Letting the deferred
part of the compensation package erode while debating the current part
is, on this evidence, exactly backwards.

\emph{Treat teaching's turnover as the price of its accessibility.}
The comparison figures reframe the profession's churn: teaching pays
66 cents on the college dollar, yet loses fewer of its members than
social work, therapy, or (now) pharmacy, and uniquely improved across
decades. Part of the reason is the same flexibility that shows up as
the birth-year spike and the revolving door: teaching is cheap to
leave \emph{and cheap to re-enter}, which makes it the college
profession most compatible with an interrupted life. A policy agenda
that treated that accessibility as an asset to be organized---formal
re-entry paths, leave that preserves attachment, part-time roles that
are careers rather than exits---would work with the grain of the
anatomy documented here.

The contribution of this paper is the anatomy itself: one instrument,
twenty-eight consecutive years, every exit routed, priced, and
modeled. On that evidence, the American teaching profession is not
hemorrhaging. It is aging, underpaid relative to its peers, uniquely
exposed to the arithmetic of family life, and steady---losing its
members to the same life events it has always lost them to, at the
same rate, while the conversation about it swings with each news
cycle. Policy should be built on the steadiness, not the swings.

\section{The instrument, audited}\label{app:instrument}

For respondents in CPS rotation groups that yield four consecutive
monthly interviews after the reference year \emph{and} a March
retrospective response, profession leaving can be classified two
independent ways: by the within-panel criterion (teaching occupation in
all four post months versus none) and by the March retrospective
question. Table~\ref{tab:confusion} cross-tabulates the two for the
pooled linked sample. Among panel-classified stayers, 96.8 percent are
retrospective stayers; among panel-classified leavers, the March
question recovers the exit or the non-teacher status in 99.2 percent
of cases; overall agreement is 99.1 percent. The March instrument, in
short, reproduces at the person level the classification a
short panel would give---while covering four times the sample and
every year since 1998.
