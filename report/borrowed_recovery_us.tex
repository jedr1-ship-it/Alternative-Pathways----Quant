\begin{center}
{\fontsize{20}{26}\selectfont\bfseries
A Borrowed Recovery?\par}
\vspace{8pt}
{\fontsize{15}{20}\selectfont\bfseries
The 2025 Teacher-Workforce Data for the United States\par}

\vspace{22pt}
{\large \href{https://sites.google.com/view/joseeliasduranroa}{José Elías
Durán Roa}\footnote{PhD Candidate in Economics, University of Alicante, and
Research Intern at the OECD Directorate for Education and Skills. E-mail\,
\href{mailto:joseelias.duranroa@ocde.org}{joseelias.duranroa@ocde.org} and

\vspace{6pt}
{\small July 2026}
\end{center}

\vspace{14pt}

The teacher exodus is over. In the year to 2025, 7.9 percent of American
public school teachers left education. The average over the fifteen years
before the pandemic was 7.7 percent; at the height of the disruption the
rate hit 10.4. Every age group has drifted back to its own historical
norm. After five years of headlines about burned-out teachers fleeing the
profession, the 2025 data sound the all-clear.

I believe the number. I do not believe the all-clear, and this essay
explains why. Nothing about teaching got better in 2025. Teacher pay
stands further behind the graduate labor market than at any point since
2010. The flow of teachers out of the classroom into the jobs next to it
is the widest on record. The typical new recruit is a forty-two-year-old
career changer, and the workforce just shrank for the first time since
the pandemic trough. What changed is the world outside the school
building: the coldest hiring market in a decade has quietly done the work
that pay and working conditions were supposed to do. Retention is being
rented from the business cycle. Rentals get recalled.

\section*{Fewer pupils, and now fewer teachers}

Start with demography, the slowest-moving piece. American public schools
have been losing students since the fall of 2019, when enrollment peaked
at 50.8 million. Smaller birth cohorts and the pandemic exodus have since
removed 1.4 million pupils, and the official projections point further
down. For five years the teaching workforce ignored this. It dipped in
2021, recovered, and by 2024 stood near its 2019 peak of 4.6 million.
Fewer children, same teachers: pupil-teacher ratios improved without
anyone designing it (Figure~1).

In 2025 the workforce caught up with its students. The stock of qualified
teachers fell from 4.54 to 4.37 million, a drop of 3.7 percent, the
sharpest one-year decline in the series outside the pandemic itself. Some
adjustment had to come. Districts staffed up on federal relief money, the
money has run out, and letting attrition shrink the payroll is how a
school system downsizes without ever using the word layoff. A workforce
falling in step with its student body is not alarming on its own. The
alarming part is the composition of the flows underneath, which is where
the rest of this essay lives.

\section*{Two ways to leave a classroom}

There are two honest ways to count a lost teacher. The narrow way asks
whether she still works anywhere in education. The broad way asks whether
she is still a classroom teacher. The distinction sounds like accounting.
It turns out to be the whole story of 2025 (Figure~2).

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{figures/br_fig2_attrition.pdf}
  \caption{Two ways to leave: out of the classroom, and out of education.}
  \fignote{Public-school full-time K--12 teachers with a bachelor's degree
  or more, followed twelve months in linked CPS interviews, 2005--2024
  base years ($n=91{,}035$; 2{,}841 in 2024, which lacks October links
  because no October 2025 survey exists). Blue: working in no education
  occupation at $t{+}12$ and not teaching in any later interview. Dashed:
  no longer a classroom teacher by the same standard. The gap between the
  lines is movement into other education work.}
\end{figure}

Counted the narrow way, the crisis is over. The 7.9 percent who left
education in 2024--25 is statistically indistinguishable from the
long-run norm. The age profile has settled with it: the under-35s, whose
exits spiked hardest after 2020, are back near their own rate, and so are
the mid-career group and the over-fifties, whose line is permanently the
highest because it carries retirements (Figure~3).

Counted the broad way, the crisis did not end. It changed address. Last
year 13.3 percent of public school teachers left the classroom, still
two points above the pre-pandemic norm, and the wedge between the two
lines of Figure~2 reached 5.3 percentage points, the widest in the twenty
years I can measure. Before the pandemic it averaged 3.4. Two of every
five teachers who left a classroom last year never left education. They
became instructional coaches, deans, counselors, tutors, college
instructors. In the sector's statistics this is churn, invisible in the
numbers that make headlines. In the school it is the same empty desk at
the front of the same room, and the person who used to stand at it is
often down the hall, earning a little more to teach nobody.

\section*{The new teacher is forty-two}

Attrition is half of the ledger. Replacement is the other half, and it is
the half that looks least like a recovery. The median teacher who entered
an American classroom between 2024 and 2025 was forty-two years old.
Three quarters arrived straight from another job, 18 percent from outside
the labor force, and fewer than one in five was under thirty. Entries
replaced 16.6 percent of the stock while exits took 20.3, which is the
arithmetic behind the shrinking workforce of Figure~1. The profession is
aging from the entry door: teachers under thirty-five were 32 percent of
the workforce in the mid-2000s and are 29 percent today.

Career changers are not worse teachers. A profession that can pull in a
forty-two-year-old engineer is healthier than one nobody re-enters, and
about one apparent leaver in six is back in a classroom within months, a
revolving door that softens every flow in this essay. But hire a
twenty-five-year-old and you buy, in expectation, three decades of
teaching; hire a forty-two-year-old and you buy half that. And the career
changer is, by definition, a person comparing two careers in real time.
She responds to relative pay the way a thermostat responds to
temperature. So what has happened to relative pay?

\section*{The pay that never came back}

A teacher at the median earned \$1,380 a week in 2025. Her counterpart in
2010 earned \$1,462, in the same 2025 dollars. Fifteen years of budgets,
strikes and salary schedules have left the median American teacher 5.6
percent poorer in real terms, while the median full-time worker gained 9
percent and the median college graduate gained 3 (Figure~4). Measured
against the graduate market in which teaching actually recruits, teacher
pay has slid from 87 cents on the graduate dollar to 79. The inflation
surge did the worst of it: between 2021 and 2023 teachers lost nearly 6
percent of their real weekly pay, and the rebound since has clawed back
less than half.

Which leaves a puzzle. Teaching pays relatively worse than at any point
in fifteen years. The classroom is leaking into the back office at a
record rate. And yet attrition from the sector sits exactly on its
long-run average. How can all three be true at once?

\section*{A recovery on loan}

Because teachers are people, and people quit jobs when there are other
jobs to go to. The left panel of Figure~5 plots the rate at which
teachers leave education against the national unemployment rate, drawn
upside down so that good times point up. The two lines breathe together.
When unemployment reached ten percent after 2008, teacher attrition sat
at the floor of its range. Through the long 2010s expansion both climbed.
Both peaked in the overheated market of 2019, collapsed with COVID, and
have now come down together. Against the private-sector quits rate, the
economy's willingness to walk out of a job, the correlation over two
decades is 0.33. Teachers quit when everyone quits.

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/br_fig6_market.pdf}
  \caption{Teacher attrition and the outside labor market.}
  \fignote{Left: teachers leaving education (as in Figure 2) and the
  national unemployment rate (right axis, inverted). Right: the same
  leaver rate against the private-sector quits rate (JOLTS, annual
  averages), with an OLS fit; the 2024 point lies on the line. The 2019
  observation links into 2020 and carries the COVID shock.}
\end{figure}

Now look at what the market has been doing. Private quits have fallen
from 3.1 percent a month in the 2022 frenzy to 2.3 percent. Hiring rates
are at their lowest in a decade. Job openings per unemployed worker are
back below their pre-pandemic level. Feed that 2.3 percent quits rate
into the historical relationship and it predicts teacher attrition of
almost exactly 8 percent. The observed rate is 7.9. The 2024 point in the
right panel of Figure~5 sits on the fitted line, to the decimal.

This is the uncomfortable explanation for the good news. Normal attrition
in 2025 is not evidence that teaching became more attractive; the pay
series says plainly that it did not. It is evidence that the alternatives
became less attractive, for now. The same fitted line implies that a
return of quits to their 2022 level would push attrition back up by
around half a point, and history says the young and the career changers,
the margins already thinnest, move first. What the labor market has lent,
the labor market can recall.

\section*{The window}

Read quickly, the 2025 data close the book on the pandemic teacher
crisis: attrition normal, every age group settled, ratios improving. Read
carefully, they describe a workforce in remission rather than in health.
The stock is falling. The classroom leaks into the jobs beside it at a
record rate. New supply is old and thin among the young. Pay is eight
points further behind the graduate market than in 2010. And the retention
holding it all together is fully explained by a slow economy.

Of those five facts, school systems control exactly one: what teaching
pays, and whether the classroom itself is a job worth keeping rather than
a stepping stone to the jobs around it. A slack labor market is the
cheapest moment there is to fix pay, because retention does not need
rescuing at the same time. That is the window the cycle has opened. The
next boom will close it, and will then run the experiment of 2019 again
on a workforce that is older, better paid everywhere else, and already
halfway out the classroom door. The 2025 data are not the end of the
story. They are the intermission.

{\footnotesize\textbf{A note on the data.} Teacher series: CPS basic
monthly microdata, January 2005 to December 2025 (Census Bureau; NBER
mirror before 2021). The CPS re-interviews each address twelve months
apart; persons are linked across interviews and validated on sex, race
and age progression, following Madrian and Lefgren (2000). Attrition
(Figures 2, 3 and 5) is measured on public-school full-time K--12
teachers, occupations 2310, 2320 and 2330, with a bachelor's degree or
higher, in base rotations that allow re-interviews. A leaver has left the
classroom (dashed line) or every education-adjacent occupation (solid
line) at $t{+}12$ and is not observed teaching in any later interview;
this definition matches the public-school leaver rates of 7.6 to 8.0
percent in the NCES Teacher Follow-up Survey and the retrospective CPS
literature (Harris and Adams, 2007; Aldeman and Yi, 2025). Stocks and
earnings use the full K--12 universe including private schools, with
final person weights throughout. External series: NCES Digest table
203.10 for enrollment; BLS via FRED for comparator earnings
(LEU0252918500, LEU0252881600), quits (JTS1000QUR), unemployment (UNRATE)
and the CPI-U deflator. No CPS was collected in October 2025 because of
the federal shutdown, so 2025 stocks average eleven months and the
2024--25 links exclude October. Replication code:
\texttt{src/30\_borrowed\_recovery\_data.py} and
\texttt{src/31\_borrowed\_recovery\_figures.py}.}
