{\fontsize{20}{26}\selectfont\bfseries
A Borrowed Recovery?\par}
\vspace{8pt}
{\fontsize{15}{20}\selectfont\bfseries
The 2025 Teacher-Workforce Data for the United States\par}

\vspace{22pt}
{\large \href{https://sites.google.com/view/joseeliasduranroa}{José Elías

\vspace{14pt}

In the year to 2025, 7.9 percent of American public school teachers left
education, a rate statistically indistinguishable from the 7.7 percent
that prevailed over the fifteen years before the pandemic, and well below
the 10.4 percent reached when schools were closing and reopening in
chaos. Every age group has drifted back toward its own historical norm.
After five years of headlines about teachers fleeing the profession, the
exodus is, by the standard measure, over.

I believe the number, which comes from following ninety thousand teachers
across linked survey interviews, one year at a time, for two decades.
What I do not believe is the comfortable reading of it. Teacher pay
stands further behind the graduate labor market than at any point since
2010, the flow of teachers out of the classroom and into the jobs around
it is the widest on record, and the typical new recruit is now a
forty-two-year-old changing careers rather than a young graduate choosing
one. Nothing about teaching became more attractive in 2025.

What became less attractive were the alternatives, once the hottest job
market in decades finally cooled. A recovery that rests on the weakness
of the competition is not a recovery so much as a loan, and this essay is
about the terms.

\section*{Fewer pupils, and now fewer teachers}

American public schools have been losing students since the fall of 2019,
when enrollment peaked at 50.8 million; smaller birth cohorts and the
pandemic exodus have since removed 1.4 million pupils, and the official
projections point further down. For five years the teaching workforce
paid no attention, dipping in 2021, recovering, and standing by 2024
nearly where it had stood at the peak. The result was a quiet improvement
in pupil-teacher ratios that nobody designed and few noticed (Figure~1).

In 2025 the workforce finally caught up with its students, falling from
4.54 to 4.37 million qualified teachers, the sharpest one-year decline in
the series outside the pandemic itself. Some of this adjustment had to
come, because districts staffed up on federal relief money that has now
run out, and letting attrition shrink the payroll is how a school system
downsizes without ever pronouncing the word layoff.

A workforce falling in step with its student body is not, on its own, a
problem. Whether it becomes one depends on who is leaving and who is
arriving, which is what the rest of the data describe.

\section*{Two ways to leave a classroom}

There are two honest ways to count a lost teacher. The narrow way asks
whether she still works anywhere in education; the broad way asks whether
she is still teaching a class. The distinction sounds like bookkeeping,
and it turns out to carry the whole story of the year (Figure~2).

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{figures/br_fig2_attrition.pdf}
  \caption{Two ways to leave: out of the classroom, and out of education.}
  \fignote{Public-school full-time K--12 teachers with a bachelor's degree
  or more, followed twelve months in linked CPS interviews, 2005--2024
  base years ($n=91{,}035$; 2{,}841 in 2024, which lacks October links
  because no October 2025 survey exists). Blue: working in no education
  occupation at $t{+}12$ and not teaching in any later interview. Dashed:
  no longer a classroom teacher by the same standard. The gap between the
  lines is movement into other education work.}
\end{figure}

Counted the narrow way, the crisis is over. Attrition from education sits
on its long-run average, and the age profile beneath it has settled as
well: the youngest teachers, whose exits rose most sharply after 2020,
are back near their own historical rate, while the over-fifties remain
structurally the highest because their line carries retirements
(Figure~3).

Counted the broad way, the crisis did not end so much as change address.
Last year 13.3 percent of public school teachers left the classroom,
roughly two points above the old norm, and the wedge between leaving the
classroom and leaving education reached its widest point in the twenty
years I can measure. Two of every five teachers who left a classroom in
2024 actually stayed in education, becoming instructional coaches, deans,
counselors, tutors or college instructors.

For the sector's payroll this is churn, invisible in the statistics that
make headlines. For the school it is the same empty desk at the front of
the same room, except that the person who used to stand at it is now down
the hall, earning slightly more to teach nobody.

\end{figure}

\section*{The new teacher is forty-two}

Attrition is only half of the ledger; the other half is replacement, and
it is the half that looks least like a recovery. The median teacher who
entered an American classroom between 2024 and 2025 was forty-two years
old, three quarters arrived directly from another job, and fewer than one
in five was under thirty. Entries replaced roughly one teacher in six
while exits removed one in five, which is the arithmetic behind the
shrinking stock of Figure~1, and the profession is aging from the entry
door: teachers under thirty-five made up 32 percent of the workforce in
the mid-2000s and 29 percent today.

None of this means career changers make worse teachers, and a profession
that a forty-two-year-old engineer still wants to join is healthier than
one nobody re-enters. Note also that about one apparent leaver in six is
back in a classroom within months, a revolving door that quietly softens
every flow in this essay.

But consider what the composition implies. Hire a twenty-five-year-old
and you buy, in expectation, three decades of future teaching; hire a
forty-two-year-old and you buy half of that. And a career changer is, by
construction, someone comparing two careers in real time, which makes the
inflow exquisitely sensitive to the one variable that has moved steadily
in the wrong direction.

\section*{The pay that never came back}

A teacher at the median earned \$1,380 a week in 2025, in dollars of that
year; her counterpart in 2010 earned \$1,462. Fifteen years of budgets,
strikes and salary schedules have therefore left the median American
teacher nearly 6 percent poorer in real terms, over a period in which the
median full-time worker gained 9 percent and the median college graduate
gained 3 (Figure~4).

Measured against the graduate market in which teaching actually recruits,
teacher pay has slid from 87 cents on the graduate dollar to 79. The
inflation of 2021 to 2023, which salary schedules were too rigid to
match, did most of the damage, and the rebound since has recovered less
than half of what those two years took.

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{figures/br_fig4_pay.pdf}
  \caption{Real median weekly earnings: teachers, all graduates, all
  workers.}
  \fignote{Teachers: weighted median usual weekly earnings of full-time
  K--12 teachers (BA+), CPS outgoing-rotation interviews, deflated by
  CPI-U. Graduates: BLS median usual weekly earnings, bachelor's degree
  and higher, age 25+. All workers: BLS real median usual weekly
  earnings, age 16+. Indexed to 2010 = 100.}
\end{figure}

So the puzzle is this. Teaching pays relatively worse than at any point
since 2010, the classroom is leaking into the back office at a record
rate, and attrition from the sector nevertheless sits precisely on its
long-run average. Something is holding teachers in place, and the pay
data say plainly that it is not the pay.

\section*{A recovery on loan}

Teachers are people, and people quit jobs when there are other jobs to go
to. The left panel of Figure~5 plots the rate at which teachers leave
education against the national unemployment rate, drawn upside down so
that good times point upward, and the two lines breathe together: when
unemployment reached ten percent after 2008, teacher attrition sat at the
floor of its range, both climbed through the long expansion of the 2010s,
and both peaked in the overheated market of 2019 before coming down
together. Against the private-sector quits rate, which measures the
economy's willingness to walk out of a job, the correlation across two
decades is 0.33. Teachers quit when everyone quits.

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/br_fig6_market.pdf}
  \caption{Teacher attrition and the outside labor market.}
  \fignote{Left: teachers leaving education (as in Figure 2) and the
  national unemployment rate (right axis, inverted). Right: the same
  leaver rate against the private-sector quits rate (JOLTS, annual
  averages), with an OLS fit; the 2024 point lies on the line. The 2019
  observation links into 2020 and carries the COVID shock.}
\end{figure}

Now consider what that market has been doing. Private quits have fallen
from 3.1 percent a month at the height of the 2022 frenzy to 2.3, hiring
is at its weakest in a decade, and vacancies per unemployed worker are
back below their pre-pandemic level. If teacher attrition simply followed
its historical relationship with quits, the cooling alone would predict a
rate of almost exactly 8 percent; the observed rate is 7.9, and the 2024
point in the right panel of Figure~5 lies on the fitted line to the
decimal.

This is the uncomfortable explanation for the good news. Attrition is
normal not because teaching recovered but because its competitors
weakened, and what a slack market gives, a tight one takes back. The same
fitted line implies that a return of quits to their 2022 level would
raise attrition by around half a point, with the young and the career
changers, the margins already thinnest, moving first.

\section*{The window}

Read quickly, the 2025 data close the book on the pandemic teacher
crisis, with attrition normal, every age group settled and ratios
improving. Read carefully, they describe a workforce in remission rather
than in health: a stock that is now falling, a classroom that leaks into
the jobs beside it, new supply that is old and thin among the young, and
pay that is eight points further behind the graduate market than in 2010,
the whole arrangement stabilized by nothing more durable than a slow
economy.

School systems control exactly one of those variables. A slack labor
market is the cheapest possible moment to restore the relative pay of
teachers, and to make the classroom a job worth keeping rather than a
stepping stone to the jobs around it, precisely because retention does
not need rescuing at the same time. The next boom will re-run the
experiment of 2019 on an older, thinner, worse-paid workforce, unless
someone uses the interval to change the terms. The 2025 data are not the
end of the story; they are the intermission.

{\footnotesize\textbf{A note on the data.} Teacher series: CPS basic
monthly microdata, January 2005 to December 2025 (Census Bureau; NBER
mirror before 2021). The CPS re-interviews each address twelve months
apart; persons are linked across interviews and validated on sex, race
and age progression, following Madrian and Lefgren (2000). Attrition
(Figures 2, 3 and 5) is measured on public-school full-time K--12
teachers, occupations 2310, 2320 and 2330, with a bachelor's degree or
higher, in base rotations that allow re-interviews. A leaver has left the
classroom (dashed line) or every education-adjacent occupation (solid
line) at $t{+}12$ and is not observed teaching in any later interview;
this definition matches the public-school leaver rates of 7.6 to 8.0
percent in the NCES Teacher Follow-up Survey and the retrospective CPS
literature (Harris and Adams, 2007; Aldeman and Yi, 2025). Stocks and
earnings use the full K--12 universe including private schools, with
final person weights throughout. External series: NCES Digest table
203.10 for enrollment; BLS via FRED for comparator earnings
(LEU0252918500, LEU0252881600), quits (JTS1000QUR), unemployment (UNRATE)
and the CPI-U deflator. No CPS was collected in October 2025 because of
the federal shutdown, so 2025 stocks average eleven months and the
2024--25 links exclude October. Replication code:
\texttt{src/30\_borrowed\_recovery\_data.py} and
\texttt{src/31\_borrowed\_recovery\_figures.py}.}
